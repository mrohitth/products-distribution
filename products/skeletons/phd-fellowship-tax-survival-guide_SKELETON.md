## 2. EMOTIONAL HOOK

You've just received your fellowship notice. The award letter says "not subject to employment taxes" and you breathe a small sigh of relief — until you read further and realize you've just been handed taxable income that your university will never withhold for you.

Here's the thing: the IRS absolutely expects taxes on fellowship money. The IRS just also expects you to figure that out on your own.

You're not irresponsible for missing this. Nobody told you. Your university has a general tax FAQ. Your PI said "congrats." Your cohort groupchat debated it once and nobody resolved anything. And now it's October and you vaguely recall something about quarterly checks and you've been quietly hoping it would somehow work itself out.

This guide is for you. It exists because the gap between what universities tell you and what the IRS expects from you is enormous, and you're supposed to fill it with no training and no support. This guide will not do that for you — but it will tell you exactly what to do, step by step, so you're not guessing at midnight on April 14th.

## 3. PRODUCT ARCHITECTURE

### PART 1: Understand What You're Actually Dealing With

**Chapter 1: Classify Your Fellowship Type Before You Do Anything Else**
→ You will be able to identify whether your fellowship counts as a scholarship, a stipend, or compensation — and know exactly which IRS code section governs each. You will complete a classification worksheet that maps your specific award letter language to the correct tax treatment. No more wondering which bucket you're in.

**Chapter 2: Identify Which Dollars Are Taxable and Which Are Not**
→ You will understand the specific rule: educational grants used for tuition and required fees are generally tax-free; anything paid for living expenses is taxable. You will run your own fellowship budget through a line-item filter and produce a clear number — your actual taxable income for the year.

**Chapter 3: Read Your Award Letter With a Tax Lens**
→ You will know what sentences in your award letter signal taxable status, which phrases are actually irrelevant to tax liability, and which parts of your offer letter create hidden obligations (like service requirements that convert a scholarship to a wage). You will never sign a fellowship agreement without knowing the tax implications first.

---

### PART 2: Solve the Quarterly Payment Problem

**Chapter 4: Calculate Your Quarterly Payment in 30 Minutes**
→ You will use a fillable worksheet that walks through your fellowship income, subtracts qualified education exclusions, applies the current quarterly estimated tax formula, and spits out a concrete payment amount for each due date. You will know what number to send to the IRS and when.

**Chapter 5: Set Up IRS Quarterly Payments From a Zero-Balance Account**
→ You will open an IRS Direct Pay account or locate the estimated tax voucher forms (Form 1040-ES) and know exactly which scenario applies to your situation. You will understand the difference between the safe harbor thresholds and the penalty threshold — and which one you're targeting.

**Chapter 3 from original spec moved into structure:**
→ You will know the four payment due dates (April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15), which ones you can skip if you qualify for the prior-year safe harbor, and what happens if you skip all of them. You will have a calendar entry system ready on day one.

---

### PART 3: File the Right Forms Without a Tax Professional

**Chapter 7: Pull the Correct 1098-T and Decode It**
→ You will know exactly what Box 1, Box 2, and Box 5 on your 1098-T actually mean for fellowship recipients, and whether your university correctly labeled your fellowship as a scholarship or a Form W-2 wage. You will identify errors on your 1098-T before the IRS does.

**Chapter 8: Complete Schedule 1 (Additional Income and Adjustments to Income)**
→ You will identify that fellowship income enters your return through Schedule 1 Line 8 ("Other income"), navigate to the correct line, and enter the right amount — with a side-by-side comparison of a $35,000 university fellowship, a $40,000 NSF GRFP, and a $50,000 federal training grant. You will file your federal return without a CPA.

**Chapter 9: Handle the State Return — Fellowship Income Version**
→ You will understand that state tax treatment of fellowship income is not the same as federal, and know which states follow IRS rules and which states have their own treatment. You will not accidentally underpay a state that considers your fellowship fully taxable when your federal filing suggests otherwise.

### PART 4: Cover the Edge Cases That Will Catch You

**Chapter 10: State-by-State Fellowship Tax Rules for the 10 Highest Grad-Student-Population States**
→ You will look up your specific state (California, New York, Texas, Massachusetts, Illinois, Pennsylvania, Michigan, North Carolina, Georgia, Florida) and get a one-page state profile showing: income tax yes/no, fellowship treatment (scholarship exclusion available or not), where to report, any special forms required. Downloadable state profile sheet included.

**Chapter 11: Multi-State Fellowship Liability — Fieldwork, Visiting Researcher, Remote Student**
→ You will know how to handle the scenario where you receive a fellowship from State A, do research in State B for four months, and your home institution is in State C. You will understand domicile vs. physical presence rules and know how to file accordingly without triggering a state notice.

**Chapter 12: Audit-Proof Your Fellowship Return Before You File**
→ You will conduct a five-point checklist against your filed return: classification is correct, exclusions are documented, payments match Form 1040-ES records, state filings are consistent with federal, and scholarship vs. compensation distinction is defensible. You will know what documentation to keep and for how long.

## 4. KEY HUMAN TOUCH ELEMENTS

- **The NSF quarterly check story**: Open or anchor with the verbatim from the signal data — "I had to send in quarterly checks to the IRS with my NSF fellowship." This is the reality check moment. Every reader who has an NSF fellowship will feel seen immediately.

- **The "but I didn't get a W-2" assumption**: Many grad students believe income without a W-2 is income they don't owe taxes on. Address this directly in Part 1. It's the most common and most dangerous assumption in this space.

- **The April 15 shock**: The moment a grad student realizes they owe $3,000–$5,000 on fellowship money they already spent. Use this as the emotional anchor in the quarterly payment section — show that it was preventable and what the specific mechanism is.

- **The institutional handoff problem**: Grad students get bounced between Bursar, HR, their PI's grants office, and the student financial services website — nobody owns the tax answer. Acknowledge this directly. Position the guide as the answer that nobody else gave you.
