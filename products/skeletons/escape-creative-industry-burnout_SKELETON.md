


# Product Skeleton

**TREND:** Creative Industry Exit Planning — Confidence Score: 10/12

---

## 1. PRODUCT TITLE

**Options (under 40 characters):**

1. *The Off-Ramp: Exit Your Creative Career Without Starting Over*
2. *After the Grind: A Skills-Based Exit Plan for Burned-Out Creatives*
3. *Not Your Dream Job: A Practical Exit Guide for Video Professionals*

**Recommendation:** #2 — leads with emotional validation (*After the Grind*) while promising specificity (*skills-based exit plan*). Positions the product as grounded and strategic, not woo-woo.

---

## 2. EMOTIONAL HOOK

You're in a 12-seat editing suite at 11pm. Again. The job pays well enough that leaving feels irresponsible, but it pays badly enough that you can't remember the last time you felt proud of anything you made. The resentment didn't arrive all at once — it crept in over late nights and missed weekends and a director who keeps saying "just one more pass." You Googled "what else can I do with editing skills" once, found a generic Reddit thread, and closed the tab. That was six months ago.

The skills you built — tight editing, client communication, fast turnaround under pressure, visual storytelling, project management, client wrangling — are rare. They are also, almost entirely, transferable. But nobody in your industry talks about this because your entire professional identity is wrapped up in "being a creator." So instead you stay, resent, burn out, and start the cycle again.

This guide won't tell you to "follow your passion" or "monetize your side hustle." It will give you a step-by-step, skills-mapped exit plan: which industries actually want what you do, how to rebrand your portfolio without lying, how to negotiate a lateral (not downward) move, and how to do it while you're still employed so you don't have to panic your way into the next thing. You built a career. Now you're going to use it to get out.

## 3. PRODUCT ARCHITECTURE

### PART 1: The Inventory — Know What You're Actually Carrying

**Chapter 1: Run a Skills Audit That Your Resume Never Included**
*Output:* Reader completes a two-column audit (technical skills + transferable soft skills) that identifies at least 8 transferable competencies beyond "I edit video." They'll have a written reference sheet they can use in every future application — no more staring at a blank "Skills" field.

**Chapter 2: Reverse-Map Your Worst Projects to Your Best Leverage Points**
*Output:* Reader maps 3–5 draining projects to the hard skills they built under pressure (e.g., "directed talent in 4 hours with no script" → stakeholder management under ambiguity). Creates a private document reframing every frustrating job as professional evidence.

**Chapter 3: Calculate Your Burnout Threshold — And Your Real Financial Number**
*Output:* Reader completes a concrete financial baseline worksheet: bare-minimum monthly number, comfortable number, and exit-savings target. Includes a "months-to-escape" calculator that tells them exactly how long they need to keep working to build a runway — making the abstract feel tractable.

---

### PART 2: The Market — Where Your Skills Are Already Wanted

**Chapter 4: The 7 Industries That Want Editors (And Don't Know It Yet)**
*Output:* Reader reviews a one-page landscape of target industries — corporate video, e-learning, UX research documentation, internal comms, nonprofit storytelling, legal/forensic video, podcast and audio editing — with salary range bands and honest gut-checks about culture fit. They'll rank these by personal fit and start with the one that requires zero new credentialing.

**Chapter 5: How to Speak Corporate in 48 Hours**
*Output:* Reader rewrites three bullet points from their existing resume using industry-adjacent language (e.g., "color graded footage" → "optimized visual assets for cross-platform distribution"). Includes a literal phrase-bank they can paste into LinkedIn, Indeed, and direct applications. They'll have a before/after resume excerpt they can show or test.

**Chapter 6: Portfolio Rehab Without a Reel Overhaul**
*Output:* Reader selects 2–3 existing pieces from their portfolio and writes three different "about me" introductions targeting three different industries — corporate, nonprofit, e-learning. They'll have a templated framework for customizing a portfolio for each lane without rebuilding anything from scratch.

---

### PART 3: The Move — Execute Without Jumping Into Empty Air

**Chapter 7: The Bridge Role Strategy — Lateral Entry, Vertical Growth**
*Output:* Reader identifies 3 specific job titles one level removed from editing (associate producer at a corporate agency, content coordinator at a mid-size brand, project coordinator at a production house) and drafts outreach scripts for informational interviews at each. They'll have a templated email they can customize and send that same week.

**Chapter 8: Build Your Side Income to Prove the Concept Before You Quit**
*Output:* Reader identifies one service offering (e.g., "editing corporate webinar recordings at $75–150/hour on Upwork or through cold outreach to 5 local businesses") and drafts a service page or Upwork profile bio optimized for their target industry. They'll know the exact first-step action to generate revenue before leaving their main job.

**Chapter 9: The Negotiation Reset — How to Take a Lateral Move and Not Feel Like You're Starting Over**
*Output:* Reader prepares a "compilation portfolio" document: a single-page reference that bundles their creative output, project management wins, and client communication track record into evidence for negotiating title and salary. Includes a salary negotiation email template with bracketed variables — they fill in the blanks and have a ready-to-send message.

---

### PART 4: The Aftermath — Actually Survive the Transition

**Chapter 10: The Identity Unpack — What You Are If You're Not a "Creator"**
*Output:* Reader writes a private 1-page "Professional Identity Statement" that defines their value proposition outside of creative martyrdom. This is a grounding document to re-read during the uncomfortable weeks when your old title feels like armor and your new role feels like a demotion.

**Chapter 11: Set Boundaries in a New Industry Before You Even Start**
*Output:* Reader drafts a personal "boundary document" — a private list of the 3–4 things they will not do again (e.g., "no 11pm client revisions," "no unpaid spec work for exposure") — and identifies the specific language to use in their first 90 days at a new job when someone asks them to violate those boundaries. Includes a script for saying no professionally.

**Chapter 12: The 6-Month Check-In — Metrics That Actually Tell You If You're Better Off**
*Output:* Reader completes a personal transition scorecard: financial wellbeing, schedule control, physical symptoms, resentment level (tracked by their own honest 1–10 scale), and skill development. They'll have a concrete review schedule and a decision tree: if X metric is still red at 6 months, here are 3 specific adjustments to make — without abandoning the whole plan.

## 4. KEY HUMAN TOUCH ELEMENTS

**Lead with the physical.** The weight gain, the insomnia, the neck pain from an edit bay chair — these aren't melodrama, they're data. Opening with the body's protest signals gives readers permission to stop treating this as a mindset problem. Framing it as the body's version of a P&L statement ("your body is sending you a financial warning") is disarming and grounded.

**Name the specific trap.** Video editors and motion designers are not burned-out generic creatives — they're people trapped by a skillset that feels hyper-specific. The product needs to acknowledge that they *know* their industry is prestigious enough to be hard to leave (music videos, commercials, film are culturally romanticized) and that wanting out doesn't mean they failed. That tension — staying because leaving feels ungrateful — is the real paralysis.

**The "skill audit" moment.** The first time someone actually lists their transferable skills on paper and sees "client communication under deadline pressure," "visual narrative for non-technical audiences," and "managing feedback from 6 stakeholders simultaneously" — that recalibration moment, where generic experience becomes concrete leverage, is the emotional peak of the product. Design the worksheets around creating that moment, not just the information.

**The bridge role is not a demotion.** This framing is essential: editors often fear that moving out of post-production means abandoning their identity. The bridge role chapter must explicitly reframe this as strategic positioning — taking a role that uses your existing skills to build new credentials while getting out from under the production grind. The product should use the word *leverage* consistently, not *settle.*

## 5. FORMAT & PRICING

**Format:** PDF guide (60–70 pages) + 8 fillable worksheets delivered as a download. Total package. No tiered membership, no upsell, no community — deliver the complete product and let it sell itself.

**Pricing Rationale:**
At $29, this competes with generic career books on Kindle. Too cheap for someone who's been making $70–90K in a major market — it reads as unserious. At $79, it sits alongside mid-tier digital courses on Domestika or Skillshare without the platform overhead. The target buyer is someone earning $60–90K who is *desperate enough to pay to solve this problem* — that person will not blink at $79 for a concrete plan. At $49 with a landing-page discount (launch price $29), you capture urgency and social proof simultaneously.

**Recommended price:** Launch $29 → permanent $49. No subscription.

## 6. MARKET INTEL ANGLE

**The broader thesis:** Creative burnout isn't a niche problem — it's a leading indicator of what happens when prestige economics and labor exploitation overlap in an industry built on the myth of "doing what you love." Post-2023, the creator economy's golden era narrative has cracked. Layoffs in streaming, contracting ad budgets, and the AI displacement conversation have made even mid-career creatives start asking hard questions about sustainability that they would have dismissed two years ago.

**Adjacent market signals:**
The personal finance and FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) community — a $500M+ media ecosystem — has done a brilliant job making "escape your job" feel aspirational. But their audience skews tech and finance. There's a massive gap in that playbook for people whose escape route is *not* about saving aggressively and investing — it's about **skills portability**. The creative exit question is: I have high-value skills in a broken system, where do those skills go? Nobody is serving that question with the specificity this audience needs.

**What this product plugs into:** It sits at the intersection of career change content (saturated), creative burnout content (oversimplified), and personal finance for creatives (thin). It wins by being the most specific thing in the room — a reader should finish the first chapter and feel like they've already gotten more value than a dozen Reddit threads. Specificity is the moat.

**Positioning for growth:** This is the anchor product for a content platform serving burned-out creative professionals. The first product proves demand; a $79 course expansion (live exercises, cohort accountability), a LinkedIn newsletter, or a community around "life after the edit suite" are all natural extensions. The PDF is the wedge.
