**Deposit Gone? Sue in Small Claims Court**

## 2. EMOTIONAL HOOK

You gave someone real money — thousands of dollars — based on a handshake and a promise. They gave you nothing. Worse than nothing: excuses, silence, maybe a half-started job that now sits as a monument to your own gullibility. You kept the contract. You kept the receipts. You have their texts. And right now, you're sitting on all this evidence, wondering if the whole thing is a lost cause.

It isn't.

Small claims court was built for exactly this situation: ordinary people wronged by ordinary businesses, with dollar amounts that don't justify hiring a lawyer but absolutely justify showing up with paperwork. The process is simpler than you think. The filing fee is lower than you fear. And the judgment — even if the contractor never pays voluntarily — becomes a legal instrument that follows them.

This guide walks you through every step: documenting the fraud, filing correctly the first time, preparing a case you can actually win, and turning a judgment into actual money. No legal jargon. No vague advice. Just the sequence of actions that gets you from "deposit stolen" to "money recovered."

## 3. PRODUCT ARCHITECTURE

### PART 1: KNOW WHAT HAPPENED TO YOU

*Quantify the loss. Document the evidence. Classify the wrong.*

**Chapter 1: Calculate Your Exact Loss and Know What You Can Claim**
You'll complete a loss worksheet that tallies your deposit, any additional payments, materials you purchased, and costs you've already incurred because of the contractor's failure. You'll also identify what you *cannot* claim in small claims court (like emotional distress or time off work) so you set realistic expectations from day one.

**Chapter 2: Preserve Every Piece of Evidence Before It Disappears**
You'll screenshot and export all text messages, emails, voicemails, and written contracts before they vanish. You'll learn which formats courts accept and how to create evidence that survives the "but my phone died" problem. You'll also identify missing documents and know exactly what written confirmation to request in your next conversation with the contractor.

**Chapter 3: Classify the Wrong — Contract Breach, Fraud, or Theft**
You'll work through a decision tree that distinguishes between a contractor who failed to deliver (breach of contract), one who never intended to deliver (fraud), and one who took your money with no plan to perform at all (theft). The classification determines which court, which filing strategy, and which outcome options apply. You'll exit this chapter knowing exactly which legal theory drives your case.

---

### PART 2: KNOW YOUR VENUE

*Understand the system before you enter it.*

**Chapter 4: Find the Right Court for Your Dollar Amount and Location**
You'll use a state-by-state guide to identify whether your county clerk, district court, or justice court handles your claim amount. You'll determine whether you file in the county where you live, where the work was supposed to happen, or where the contractor's business is registered. You'll exit this chapter knowing exactly where to go and when — because filing in the wrong court means starting over.

**Chapter 5: Model the Economics Before You File**
You'll complete a recovery calculator that weighs the filing fee against your claim amount, estimates your time investment in hours, and helps you decide whether small claims is the right path — or whether a demand letter or attorney letter might recover the money faster and cheaper. You'll understand the break-even point. You'll also learn about fee-shifting rules in your state: whether you can recover your filing fee if you win.

**Chapter 6: Map the Timeline from Filing to Hearing**
You'll receive a realistic calendar of what happens after you file: how long the court has to serve the defendant, when you'll receive your hearing date, and what deadlines apply. You'll plan your prep schedule accordingly. You'll also learn what "continuances" are and when you can or cannot request them.

---

### PART 3: BUILD A CASE YOU CAN WIN

*Structure your evidence. Draft your claim. Show up prepared.*

**Chapter 7: Draft a Statement of Claim That Judges Can Follow**
You'll use a template that walks you through describing: what you hired the contractor to do, what you paid and when, what they failed to do and when you discovered it, what attempts you've made to resolve it, and what you're asking the court to award. You'll learn to write in plain language — no legal citations, no emotional language — because judges read dozens of these. Clarity wins.

**Chapter 8: Organize Your Evidence Binder for the Hearing**
You'll build a physical and digital folder with labeled tabs: contract, payments, communications, attempts to resolve, photographs, damage estimates. You'll know how many copies the court requires and bring an extra set for yourself. You'll practice walking through the binder aloud so you can reference documents smoothly when it's your turn to speak.

**Chapter 9: Prepare for the Hearing — Questions, Objections, and Your Script**
You'll anticipate the questions the judge will ask and the questions the contractor might raise. You'll prepare three-minute and ten-minute versions of your opening statement. You'll know which objections you can make ("hearsay," "irrelevant") and which ones are not worth your credibility. You'll also plan for the contractor's most likely defense ("I intended to come back," "The homeowner changed their mind") and have responses ready.

---

### PART 4: WIN AND COLLECT

*Turn the judgment into real money.*

**Chapter 10: Serve the Defendant and Secure Your Default Win**
You'll learn how to serve legal documents properly — because improper service can undo everything. You'll understand your options: sheriff service, certified mail, process server. You'll also know what happens if the defendant doesn't show up: the default judgment process, and what to do next if they suddenly appear and try to reopen the case.

**Chapter 11: Pursue Collection Even When They Don't Pay Voluntarily**
You'll learn the three most effective collection levers: bank levies, wage garnishment, and property liens. You'll understand which levers apply based on whether the contractor owns real estate or has known bank accounts. You'll also know which collection methods require additional court steps and which you can pursue directly.

**Chapter 12: Use the Judgment as a Negotiating Tool**
You'll learn how to craft a demand letter that references the judgment and specifies what happens next if payment isn't received. You'll understand why contractors often settle immediately after a judgment — because the alternative is worse. You'll also know how to negotiate a payment plan that gets you money faster than pursuing the next collection step.

## 4. KEY HUMAN TOUCH ELEMENTS

**The Anchor Story**: Open with a composite scenario — a homeowner who paid $8,500 upfront for a kitchen renovation, found the contractor vanished after demo, spent three weeks trying to get a response, and then spent four months paralyzed before doing anything. This isn't villainizing the reader; it's showing them their exact hesitation in the mirror.

**The "Use a Friend's Phone" Moment**: Reference the verbatim quote that surfaces everywhere in contractor fraud discussions: the improvised, slightly desperate workaround someone tries when they realize their contractor has stopped answering their real number. Use this as a pivot point: *That instinct — to document, to escalate, to make contact through alternative channels — that's the right instinct. This guide is the structured version of that impulse.*

**The "Is It Even Worth It?" Calculation**: The single most common hesitation is the belief that winning won't result in getting paid. Address this head-on with the honest answer: *Sometimes you win and still don't collect. But the judgment doesn't expire. And the contractor who knows you have one is a contractor who takes your phone calls. Use it as leverage first.*

## 5. FORMAT & PRICING

**Format**: PDF guide + fillable worksheet bundle
Primary document: ~45-page comprehensive guide
Worksheets: 6 forms (Loss Calculator, Evidence Checklist, Claim Template, Evidence Binder Labels, Opening Statement Template, Demand Letter Template)

**Price Point Rationale**: $27–$37
This positioning serves the psychological reality of the audience: they've already lost real money and are evaluating whether recovery is worth pursuing. A sub-$40 investment feels proportionate to the potential recovery (often $2,000–$15,000) and signals that the guide contains genuine expertise, not generic advice. It also survives the "I could find this free" objection — because they won't find this structured, state-specific guidance for free.

**Justification for PDF over course**: This audience wants answers, not accountability coaching. They're past the "build your confidence" phase. They want to read step-by-step, reference it at filing time, and print the worksheet they need. A static PDF respects their urgency and serves their actual use case: read → print → file.

## 6. MARKET INTEL ANGLE

**Life Infrastructure Thesis**: Homeownership is the dominant form of wealth-building for most Americans, and contractor relationships are the single highest-friction interaction most homeowners have with the financial system. Deposit theft isn't a niche problem — it's a predictable consequence of an under-regulated industry where licensing requirements vary wildly, insurance verification is optional, and the barrier to creating a "company" is a website and a truck.

**The Structural Gap**: The information asymmetry here is extreme. Contractors understand deposits; most homeowners have done this once or twice. The legal system exists; most homeowners have never used it. This guide collapses that gap — not by becoming a lawyer, but by learning the specific rules of one system: small claims court.

**Why This Converts**: The search intent is immediate and monetizable. "How do I sue a contractor in small claims court" is someone with a case already built in their head, money already lost, and the decision already made to act. This isn't a person discovering a possibility; it's a person who needs a roadmap to execute a plan they've already committed to.
