# Product Skeleton

## Your PhD Tax Guide: Know What Actually Applies

---

### 1. Emotional Hook

Tax season hits differently when your income comes from a fellowship stipend, a teaching assistantship, and a research assistantship — all in the same calendar year. You've probably already fallen down a Reddit thread where someone confidently said you need a W-2, someone else insisted it's the 1098-T, and a third person mentioned form 8843, and now you're more confused than when you started.

Here's what nobody tells you: the standard tax advice for employees doesn't map cleanly onto grad student income. Your institution reports some money on a W-2, some on a 1098-T, and some not at all — and it matters enormously which is which. Getting this wrong means either a surprise tax bill or hours wasted on forms that don't apply to your situation.

This guide is built for you: a PhD student in your first, second, or third year who's never had to untangle fellowship income from wages before. By the time you're done, you'll know exactly which forms you need, where your income shows up, and how to file confidently — not because you read the IRS instructions, but because someone explained it in the language of your actual life.

---

### 2. Product Architecture

**PART 1: Map Your Income**

**Chapter 1: Identify Every Dollar Coming In**
You will be able to list all your income streams for the tax year, categorize each as stipend, fellowship, wages, or scholarship, and explain why the label matters for tax purposes. You'll stop treating your fellowship like "free money" and start seeing it as taxable income with specific reporting requirements.

**Chapter 2: Find Your W-2 and 1098-T in Three Clicks**
You will locate your W-2 in your institution's payroll portal (usually under "tax documents" or "compensation") and locate your 1098-T in the financial aid or student accounts portal. You will understand that these two forms report different money from different sources — and you need both.

**Chapter 3: Decode What Your Institution Actually Reported**
You will read your W-2 and 1098-T to extract the exact amounts your institution reported to the IRS, cross-reference them against your pay stubs to ensure nothing's missing, and identify whether your fellowship income appears on either form or not at all (which is legal for some institutional fellowships).

---

**PART 2: Build Your Filing Stack**

**Chapter 4: The Forms You'll Actually Touch**
You will know the exact purpose of the 1040, Schedule OI (for fellowship income), and form 8843 (for nonresident aliens), and in what order to complete them. You'll stop wondering if you're supposed to "just use the 1098-T" and start seeing these forms as a system with a specific sequence.

**Chapter 5: Where Fellowship Income Goes**
You will report fellowship grants on the correct line of your federal return, apply the scholarship exemption correctly (if eligible), and determine whether your fellowship counts as "qualified scholarship" for tax purposes. You'll stop guessing whether your stipend is taxable — you'll have a definite answer.

**Chapter 6: File a Zero-Filer Return if Needed**
You will determine whether you're required to file a federal return (based on income thresholds and visa status), and if your income is below the filing threshold, complete the minimal compliant filing that protects you from future IRS inquiries. You'll stop being paralyzed by "do I even need to file?"

---

**PART 3: Handle the Tricky Situations**

**Chapter 7: Report Teaching and Research Assistant Income**
You will correctly categorize RA/TA income as wages (W-2), claim any applicable tax treaty benefits if you're an international student, and report this income separately from fellowship income on your return.

**Chapter 8: Manage Multiple Income Streams in One Year**
You will handle the tax implications when you transition from a fellowship to a assistantship mid-year, or when you receive a one-time travel grant or research allowance, and determine whether you owe estimated taxes or can rely on withholding.

**Chapter 9: Claim Deductions You're Actually Eligible For**
You will identify which grad student expenses are deductible (equipment required by your program, professional development, moving for the fellowship if applicable), and avoid the common mistake of claiming personal living expenses as education deductions. You'll keep records that the IRS would accept if audited.

---

**PART 4: File and Move On**

**Chapter 10: Choose Your Filing Method**
You will decide between Free File, your institution's recommended software, or a paid platform based on your income complexity, and complete your filing in under two hours if you prepared according to parts 1-3. You'll stop cycling between TurboTax screens wondering if you're doing it right.

**Chapter 11: Check Your Withholding for Next Year**
You will use the IRS withholding calculator to adjust your W-4 if you're consistently getting large refunds or owing unexpected amounts, and understand why PhD students often over-or-under withhold because their income doesn't fit the standard assumptions.

**Chapter 12: Build Your Tax Folder for Next Year**
You will set up a simple folder system (physical or digital) that collects pay stubs, fellowship award letters, and receipts throughout the year so next January isn't a forensic archaeology project. You'll have a checklist that takes five minutes to run each December.

### 3. Key Human Touch Elements

- **The "my first year" framing**: Every complex section opens with a first-person moment ("Your first January as a PhD student, you open your inbox to find two tax documents from two different departments...") to normalize the confusion and signal that the reader isn't missing something obvious.

- **The 8843 moment**: Explicitly address the international student experience — many guides treat this as an afterthought, but form 8843 is required reading for F-1/J-1 students with fellowship income, and confusion here creates real filing errors.

- **The fellowship ambiguity**: Write a short case study showing how the same $28,000 fellowship can be reported three different ways depending on the institution's structure — and what to do in each case. This directly addresses the quote's confusion about W-2 vs 1098-T.

- **The "you don't need a CPA" conclusion**: End the guide with a clear decision tree: "If your situation is [X], file yourself. If it's [Y], make an appointment at your institution's tax clinic. If it's [Z], call a CPA." Grad students overpay for tax help they could have done themselves; this gives them permission to trust their own work.

---

### 4. Format & Pricing

**Format:** PDF guide (55-65 pages) + one companion spreadsheet (pre-labeled tabs for income tracking, expense logging, and document checklist). No video content — this audience reads on their own timeline, often at 11pm before a deadline.

**Delivery:** Downloadable immediately after purchase, organized by part so readers can work sequentially or jump to the section that matches their current panic.

**Price point rationale:** $19. This audience is living on stipends. The reference point is not "tax software subscription" (which costs $0-75) but "a friend who went through this last year and explained it over coffee." Price at $19 positions it as accessible but credible — not a $7 PDF that feels incomplete. Higher than $25 and it starts feeling like a luxury for students who need it most.

---

### 5. Market Intel Angle

PhD students are a defined, underserved niche with high stakes and no good resources. The income is real (stipends range from $20K-$45K), the tax complexity is real (multiple income types, potential visa complications, non-standard employment), and the existing content is either too general (TurboTax's "tax tips for grad students" is a 300-word blog post) or too technical (IRS Publication 970 assumes you can parse tax code).

This connects to a broader life infrastructure thesis: early-career knowledge gaps create disproportionate stress for people who are already resource-constrained. The "financial DIY" content market is growing because institutions don't provide this education and students discover the gap only when they're panicking. A PhD-specific tax guide occupies a clear niche with no direct competitors — it's not "tax help for millennials" with a PhD skin; it's built from the specific income structures of graduate education.

The audience is also unusually self-selecting for high-intent purchasers: they're not browsing, they're Googling "1098-T fellowship PhD taxes" at 1am because their filing deadline is in two weeks. This is a conversion-friendly market with low content supply and high demand.
