# Product Skeleton

**Product Title:** What You Actually Owe: Tax Estimation for Grad Fellows

---

## 1. Emotional Hook

You got the fellowship. You figured out the health insurance. You've survived qualifying exams. But then: the first stipend lands, and your paystub reads like another language. Taxable? Non-taxable? What even is a 1098-T and why do you have three of them?

You're not alone. Most grad fellows receive income structured in ways that no W-2 employee ever encounters—fellowships with tuition waivers, research assistantships with conditional tax treatment, stipends that may or may not be taxable depending on how your university coded them. The result: you either hand over $100-200 to a preparer (and cross your fingers they actually understand grad student finances) or you open TurboTax and feel a spike of genuine panic around page 3.

This guide won't turn you into an accountant. But it will give you a concrete, step-by-step system for estimating what you actually owe—before you file. You'll stop guessing. You'll stop overpaying out of fear. You'll know whether your situation is simple enough to DIY or complex enough to warrant professional help.

---

## 2. Product Architecture

### PART 1: Decode Your Income (What Money Are You Actually Receiving?)

**Chapter 1: Read Your Award Letter Like a Contract**
You'll identify which components of your funding package are taxable vs. non-taxable. You will extract the three numbers that matter—stipend amount, tuition waiver value, and any service requirement—and map them to IRS categories.

**Chapter 2: Map Every Income Stream to Its Tax Treatment**
You'll create a personalized income inventory (fellowship, GA/TA/RA stipend, hourly research,奖学金) with clear labels for each. You will distinguish between wages (W-2), fellowship income (1099 or not issued), and qualified scholarships (1098-T).

**Chapter 3: Calculate Your Tax Base**
You'll determine the exact dollar amount that counts as your taxable income—not the number on your offer letter, but the number the IRS actually looks at. You will apply the fellowship/education exclusion rules to your specific situation.

---

### PART 2: Navigate the Forms (What the IRS Will Ask About)

**Chapter 4: Match Your Forms to the Money**
You'll identify every tax form your university will send you (W-2, 1099, 1098-T, 1095-C) and explain what each one reports. You will know exactly which box on which form corresponds to each dollar of income you received.

**Chapter 5: Handle the Tuition Waiver Puzzle**
You'll determine whether your tuition waiver is taxable, partially taxable, or excluded—and what documentation you need to defend your position. You will complete the worksheet that calculates your "qualified education assistance" exclusion, if applicable.

**Chapter 6: Report Research Stipends and Service Assistantships**
You'll determine whether your RA/TA/GA work triggers wage treatment (W-2) or fellowship treatment (1099 or no form), and why that distinction matters. You will complete the income classification matrix for your specific assistantship type.

---

### PART 3: Estimate Your Tax (What You'll Actually Owe)

**Chapter 7: Calculate Annual Tax Liability**
You'll run a basic tax estimate using the standard deduction, your income classification results, and the current tax brackets. You will produce a written estimate (not a guess) of what you'll owe for the year.

**Chapter 8: Set Up Quarterly Estimated Payments**
You'll determine whether you're required to make quarterly estimated tax payments and, if so, calculate the correct amounts and due dates. You will set up a calendar system (digital or paper) with your specific deadlines.

**Chapter 9: Build a Tax Reserve Plan**
You'll calculate the monthly amount to set aside from each stipend payment to cover your annual tax liability. You will build a simple savings buffer so you never face a surprise tax bill you can't pay.

---

### PART 4: Execute (File, Hire Help, or Both)

**Chapter 10: Decide When to Hire a Professional**
You'll evaluate your specific complexity level against a rubric (number of income sources, tuition waiver value, state with no reciprocity, international status) and decide whether DIY software is sufficient or professional help is warranted. You will know the specific questions to ask a preparer to verify they understand grad student finances.

**Chapter 11: Use Tax Software Correctly for Grad Income**
You'll navigate the specific screens in TurboTax or similar software where grad fellow income is commonly mishandled. You will know exactly which answer to select for fellowship vs. scholarship vs. wages and why.

**Chapter 12: Final Review and Common Corrections**
You'll run a 12-point checklist against your completed return to catch the errors most likely to trigger notices. You will know what to do if your university made a coding error on your W-2 or 1099.

## 3. Key Human Touch Elements

**Opening anecdote:** The fellowship offer letter that said "total package value: $65,000" but deposited only $22,000—the financial aid office's silence on why. Every grad fellow has a version of this story.

**Pivot moment:** Discovering that tuition waivers aren't free money in the IRS's eyes (even when they're tax-free for you). The confusion when you realize you can't claim the American Opportunity Credit because your university already claimed the tuition benefit.

**Closing credibility:** The person three cohorts ahead who figured it out and stopped overpaying the IRS out of pure uncertainty. You're becoming that person for your cohort.

**Language to use:** Stipend, award letter, tuition waiver, 1098-T, 1099, W-2, qualified education assistance, standard deduction, quarterly estimates, Form 8812 (if relevant).

**Language to avoid:** "Game-changer," "unlock your potential," "journey," anything that sounds like it's selling a lifestyle rather than solving a specific financial problem.

---

## 4. Format & Pricing

**Format:** PDF (not video). Grad fellows prefer skimmable written content they can reference at their desk while looking at their actual paystub. Include:

- 4 fillable worksheets (income inventory, tax calculation, quarterly payment tracker, prep checklist)
- 1 reference card: "What Form Reports What"
- 1 decision tree: DIY vs. Hire a Pro

**Pricing:** $27
Rationale: Below the cost of a single tax prep session ($100-200), but clearly worth paying to avoid both overpayment and penalty risk. Anchored against the "first year I'll pay someone" behavior—price the guide as what you use *before* you decide whether to pay someone.

## 5. Market Intel Angle

Grad students are the largest unpaid labor force in American higher education, and the tax system was never built for them. Fellowship income, tuition waivers, and assistantship structures create filing complexity that standard personal finance content ignores entirely. The result: grad fellows either overpay out of confusion, underpay and face penalties, or pay preparers who don't understand their situation.

This guide positions you as the person who *did* understand—not a generic tax coach, but a resource that knows exactly how a $32,000 stipend with a $28,000 tuition waiver actually gets reported on a 1040. It's specific enough to be trustworthy, structured enough to be actionable, and niche enough to be the definitive answer in a space where nothing currently exists.
