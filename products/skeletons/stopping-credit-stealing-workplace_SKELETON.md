# Product Skeleton

**Get The Credit You're Owed**

---

## 2. Emotional Hook

You've been there. The meeting where your manager takes your analysis, presents it as his own, and gets the nod of approval from leadership. The email thread where a colleague claims the solution you built. The casual conversation where your idea gets attributed to someone who wasn't even in the room. And the worst part isn't just the stolen credit—it's the voice in your head that starts to wonder: *Maybe I don't actually contribute that much. Maybe I'm replaceable. Maybe I imagined it.*

That doubt is the real damage. It's the residue of a situation that wasn't your fault, and it doesn't belong to you.

This isn't about "speaking up more" or "having confidence." It's about a specific, solvable problem: someone took something that was yours and presented it as theirs, and you didn't have a plan for what to do next.

This is the playbook for that next time. You'll learn exactly how to respond in the moment, how to build a record that protects you going forward, and how to shift the power dynamic so it happens less often. The goal isn't to catch them—it's to make sure your work is clearly, unmistakably yours.

## 3. Product Architecture

### Part One: Ground Yourself First

**Chapter 1: Confirm It Actually Happened**
You'll learn to distinguish between normal collaboration (where ideas get mixed) and actual credit theft (where your specific contribution is claimed by someone else). You'll audit the specific incident by examining what was actually said, what was actually yours, and whether intent was present. You'll complete the "Credit Audit Worksheet" to determine if what you experienced meets the threshold for action.

**Chapter 2: Disentangle Your Contribution from Your Doubt**
You'll learn why the psychological damage of credit theft is separate from the practical damage—and why addressing only the practical damage leaves you vulnerable. You'll identify your three most significant documented contributions from the past six months using the "Evidence Inventory." You'll reframe the incident by separating "someone misrepresented my work" from "I don't contribute value."

**Chapter 3: Determine the Response Threshold**
You'll learn the four factors that determine which response is appropriate: the stakes of the theft, the relationship with the person, your power position, and whether this is a pattern or an isolated incident. You'll map your specific situation against the "Response Decision Matrix" to determine whether immediate action, documentation, or strategic patience is the right move.

---

### Part Two: Immediate Response Tactics

**Chapter 4: The In-the-Moment Recovery**
You'll learn the three-part verbal recovery framework: acknowledge the contribution without attacking the person, reestablish your involvement without appearing defensive, and redirect to next steps without escalating. You'll practice the exact phrasing for four scenarios: a manager claiming your work in a meeting, a colleague taking credit in an email, a peer claiming your solution in front of leadership, and a verbal idea theft in a brainstorm. You'll use the "Recovery Phrase Bank" worksheet to prepare scripts for your specific situation.

**Chapter 5: The 24-Hour Follow-Up**
You'll learn why the 24 hours after a credit theft are critical and what to send (and not send) in written form. You'll compose a follow-up email that accomplishes three things: documents your involvement factually, creates a written record without being accusatory, and reestablishes your ongoing role. You'll use the "Follow-Up Email Templates" to craft your version, starting from a template that matches your relationship dynamic.

**Chapter 6: When to Let It Go—Strategically**
You'll learn to distinguish between "letting it go because you're over it" (healthy) and "letting it go because you're afraid" (damaging). You'll apply the "Strategic Non-Response Framework" to decide when a minor credit theft is better handled by building a longer-term record than by immediate confrontation. You'll identify the specific conditions under which silence now creates leverage later.

---

### Part Three: Build Your Documentation System

**Chapter 7: Create Your Contribution Log**
You'll learn why the best time to document your work is before anyone disputes it—and how to build this as a five-minute weekly habit. You'll set up a simple system (digital or analog) that tracks what you created, when you created it, and who witnessed it. You'll use the "Contribution Log Template" to establish your first entries and identify the documentation gaps in your current work history.

**Chapter 8: Establish Ownership Publicly (and Subtly)**
You'll learn to embed documentation into your normal workflow: email timestamps, documented meetings, shared files with creation metadata, and verbal check-ins that establish you were there first. You'll practice three "innocuous documentation" tactics: the email that timestamps your idea, the shared doc that shows your authorship, and the verbal update that establishes your involvement. You'll complete the "Documentation Audit" to identify which of your normal channels are already creating a record you could leverage.

**Chapter 9: Build Your Witnesses System**
You'll learn why the testimony of a credible third party is more valuable than any document—and how to cultivate witnesses without appearing paranoid. You'll identify three to five people who regularly see your work who could testify to your contributions. You'll use the "Witness Cultivation Checklist" to build authentic relationships that create natural opportunities for your work to be seen.

---

### Part Four: Shift the Power Dynamic

**Chapter 10: Make Yourself Difficult to Replace**
You'll learn why credit theft targets people who are perceived as interchangeable—and how to shift that perception. You'll identify your three most high-value, irreplaceable contributions and learn to make them visible. You'll use the "Value Multiplier Framework" to ensure leadership understands what would be lost if you weren't there—not as bragging, but as operational clarity.

**Chapter 11: Negotiate Credit Explicitly**
You'll learn to name credit structures in the language of work, not the language of fairness: role definitions, authorship conventions, and contribution acknowledgments that are built in before the work is done. You'll practice the "Credit Conversation" with your manager or team lead—scheduling a 15-minute conversation about how contributions will be documented going forward. You'll use the "Credit Conversation Script" to prepare for this in a way that positions you as organized, not aggrieved.

**Chapter 12: Know When to Escalate (and How)**
You'll learn the formal channels available to you—HR, skip-level conversations, performance review documentation—and the specific conditions under which each is appropriate. You'll identify the evidence threshold you need before escalating and the realistic outcomes of each path. You'll use the "Escalation Decision Tree" to determine whether formal action makes sense for your situation, and if so, how to document and present your case without appearing retaliatory.

## 4. Key Human Touch

**Opening anecdote:** The reader should feel seen immediately with a scenario that sounds like something they lived. I want to describe a specific meeting scene—something like a quarterly review where you presented the strategy deck you built, and the director thanked your manager for "that excellent thinking" while you sat two feet away. The reader needs to feel that the author *gets it* before they're asked to read another word.

**The frame that lands:** This is the book for people who are good at their jobs and bad at taking credit. The audience for this isn't people who lack confidence in general—they often have plenty of confidence in private. They believe in their own work. They're proud of what they contribute. And that's exactly what makes credit theft so disorienting: they're not questioning whether they did good work; they're questioning whether anyone will ever know.

**Closing frame:** The goal isn't to "win" against the person who took credit. The goal is to reach a place where your work is documented, your value is visible, and the next time someone tries to present your contribution as theirs, you have a plan—and the documentation to back it up. You're not just reacting anymore. You're building a record that makes theft increasingly difficult and increasingly costly.

**Primary Product:** PDF guide, approximately 35 pages, with a clean layout that can be referenced quickly—not a novel, a manual. Readers should be able to find the chapter that matches their current situation within seconds.

**Included worksheets:**

- Credit Audit Worksheet (Part 1)
- Recovery Phrase Bank (Part 2)
- Follow-Up Email Templates (Part 2)
- Strategic Non-Response Framework (Part 2)
- Contribution Log Template (Part 3)
- Documentation Audit (Part 3)
- Witness Cultivation Checklist (Part 3)
- Credit Conversation Script (Part 4)
- Escalation Decision Tree (Part 4)

**Total package:** PDF guide + 9 worksheets, delivered as a downloadable zip

**Price point rationale:** $27 is appropriate here. The audience is experiencing a problem that costs them real career value—promotions, raises, reputation, leverage. $27 is a low-stakes investment relative to the damage of one unaddressed credit theft. It's not positioned as "cheap" (which would undermine the value signal) but it's not a barrier. The worksheets make it feel tangible and tools-forward, which justifies the price over a simple PDF.

## 6. Market Intel

This product sits at the intersection of two durable trends:

**The "My Job Is Fine But My Workplace Isn't" thesis.** The macroemployment picture has shifted. Mass layoffs have slowed, but quiet attrition of engagement and trust continues. People aren't quitting their jobs—they're quitting their willingness to invest in workplaces that don't recognize them. Credit theft is a specific, concrete manifestation of that recognition failure. Products that help people navigate the politics of their current job (rather than escape it) are positioned for a long cycle.

**The documentation-as-career-infrastructure trend.** The most durable career advice of the past five years has been about building receipts—documentation, records, proof of contributions. This is an extension of that pattern into a specific, high-stakes scenario. The audience is already primed for this framework; they're just waiting for someone to give them the exact tactics.
