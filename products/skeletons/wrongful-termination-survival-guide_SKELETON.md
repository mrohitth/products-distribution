# Product Skeleton

## 1. Product Title

**Build Your Wrongful Termination Case**

*(36 characters)*

---

## 2. Emotional Hook

You just got fired. Maybe you saw it coming. Maybe you didn't. What you do know is that something felt wrong about it—the timing, the reasons they gave, the way your complaints got you nothing but a target on your back.

Now you're sitting with the aftermath: no job, no clarity, and a growing suspicion that what happened to you was illegal. You're angry. You're also probably exhausted from trying to figure out what to do next. Maybe you've Googled "wrongful termination" a dozen times and landed on pages that say "contact an attorney" without telling you what to actually *do* while you're figuring that out.

Here's the thing: there's a window. Statutes of limitation exist, and evidence disappears—Slack messages get deleted, managers forget, companies restructure their paper trails. The worst thing you can do right now is freeze.

This guide won't replace an attorney. What it will do is give you a concrete, sequenced framework to document what happened, file your complaints correctly, and build a case file that actually means something when you do sit down with a lawyer—or if you ever need to represent yourself. You don't have to figure this out alone, and you don't have to start from zero.

## 3. Product Architecture

### Part 1: Preserve Everything (Before It Disappears)

**Chapter 1: Audit and Secure Your Digital Footprint**
Readers will export and save all work-related communications (emails, Slack/Teams messages, performance reviews) to personal storage *before* they lose access. They'll learn exactly what systems to screenshot, how to document deletion attempts, and what constitutes "destruction of evidence" in legal terms.

**Chapter 2: Build Your Chronological Evidence File**
Readers will construct a dated, categorized folder system that organizes every relevant document—termination letter, performance records, HR correspondence, complaint emails, witness threads. They'll produce a master timeline that shows the sequence of events with source citations.

**Chapter 3: Identify and Contact Witnesses While Memory Is Fresh**
Readers will list every person who witnessed relevant events, know what questions to ask them, and understand how to properly document witness statements without leading or intimidating. They'll leave with a witness contact log and a script for initial outreach.

---

### Part 2: Know What You Have (Legal Framework)

**Chapter 4: Identify Which Laws Were Violated**
Readers will match their situation to specific legal claims (Title VII, ADEA, ADA, FMLA, state-specific statutes) based on facts—not feelings. They'll determine whether their termination involved protected classes, protected activity, or contractual violations, and document the evidence supporting each potential claim.

**Chapter 5: Calculate Your Deadline (It Matters More Than You Think)**
Readers will identify the exact statute of limitations for their claim(s) and jurisdiction, accounting for tolling rules and cross-filing requirements. They'll create a deadline calendar with buffer time built in—knowing precisely when they need to file, not approximately.

**Chapter 6: Assess Case Viability Before Investing**
Readers will evaluate the strength of their evidence using a structured scoring framework (documentary evidence weight, witness corroboration, employer history, temporal proximity). They'll understand the difference between "I think I have a case" and "this is legally viable"—and know when to cut losses versus when to proceed.

---

### Part 3: File Correctly (Administrative Layer)

**Chapter 7: File With the EEOC (The Right Way)**
Readers will complete the EEOC charge form with a compelling, legally-relevant narrative that protects their options for later litigation. They'll understand what "cross-filing" means, how to request a Right to Sue letter, and what the EEOC process actually looks like—not the internet version.

**Chapter 8: File With State and Local Agencies (If Applicable)**
Readers will determine whether their state has additional protected classes or stronger remedies, file parallel complaints where strategic, and understand how state-level findings interact with federal claims. They'll avoid the common error of filing only with one agency when jurisdiction overlaps.

**Chapter 9: Draft Your Intake Narrative**
Readers will write a first-person chronological account of events formatted for legal review—clear, factual, and organized by incident rather than emotion. They'll produce a 2-3 page narrative that an attorney can actually use, structured to highlight legally significant moments.

---

### Part 4: Build for Resolution (Moving Forward)

**Chapter 10: Prepare a Discovery-Ready Case File**
Readers will assemble a complete physical and digital case file organized by claim element—ready to hand to an attorney or use in arbitration. They'll know exactly what to include, what to exclude, and how to index everything for rapid retrieval.

**Chapter 11: Prepare for Attorney Consultation (And Vet Effectively)**
Readers will identify the right type of attorney (employment, civil rights, labor), prepare specific questions to assess competence and conflict of interest, and understand fee structures (contingency vs. hourly). They'll leave consultation with a clear decision framework—not more confusion.

**Chapter 12: Manage the Emotional and Financial Timeline**
Readers will map out the realistic timeline of resolution (months to years), understand cash flow needs during litigation, and build a coping framework that acknowledges the psychological weight of pursuing a case. They'll have a personal sustainability plan that doesn't sacrifice their health for the process.

## 4. Key Human Touch Elements

**The Retaliation Fear Factor**
The guide must acknowledge that many readers are afraid of being seen as "difficult" or "that person who sued." This fear is common and often paralyzing. The framing: documenting and filing protects *you*, and in most jurisdictions, retaliating against someone for filing is its own separate violation. You're not being difficult. You're being legally prudent.

**The "Is This Even Worth It?" Moment**
Every reader hits a wall where they wonder if their case is strong enough to justify the emotional toll. The guide should validate this feeling while providing a clear decision tree: if you have documentary evidence of the discriminatory/retaliatory act, and a protected class or protected activity, and your timeline is within limits—the question isn't "do I have a case?" but "is this case worth pursuing?" That's a personal math problem, not a legal one, and this guide helps readers do that math.

**The Paperwork Paralysis Problem**
Many readers are overwhelmed by forms and don't know where to start. The guide should treat this as normal and provide templates, examples of what "good" looks like, and explicit reassurance that imperfect documentation is better than none. The enemy is perfectionism, not incompleteness.

## 5. Format & Pricing

**Format:** PDF + printable worksheets (Google Docs/Notion-ready templates for evidence logs, deadline trackers, and intake narrative drafts)

**Rationale:** This audience needs something they can *use* immediately while stressed and potentially without consistent computer access. PDF works. But the worksheets need to be structured for real humans dealing with real cognitive load—clear headers, fill-in blanks, minimal prose. Think intake form, not textbook.

**Price Point:** $27–$37

This is positioned as "competent first step before spending $10,000 on a retainer." The price signals seriousness without gatekeeping. It's not free (which would signal low value and invite people who won't actually do the work). It's not $150 (which puts it in competition with attorney consultations). It's the "I need to get my act together before I talk to a lawyer" tier.

## 6. Market Intel Angle

**Broader thesis:** Employment law is becoming contested terrain. DEI rollbacks, return-to-office mandates being used as constructive termination, and rising pro-worker sentiment in juries post-2020 have created a cohort of people who *know* something was wrong with how they lost their job and are done being gaslit about it.

**The product fits into a larger "legal self-advocacy" ecosystem**—not replacing attorneys, but positioning readers as better clients when they do engage legal help. Better-documented cases mean faster resolution, lower attorney hours, and better outcomes. This guide is the friction-reduction layer between "I got fired" and "I hired a lawyer."

**Tailwinds:** Remote work has complicated jurisdiction and created new documentation trails. Restructurings are generating waves of potential wrongful terminations. There's a growing audience of mid-career professionals who are articulate, organized, and unwilling to accept "that's just how it works" as an answer.
