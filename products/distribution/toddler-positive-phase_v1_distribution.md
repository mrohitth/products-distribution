Title: The phrase "use your words" doesn't work until you teach them the words first

Body:

I spent the first 18 months of my kid's toddler phase starting every correction with "no" and "stop." That is what I thought parenting was. Stop this. Don't touch that. No more snacks.

Then I learned something about how toddler brains process language.

When you say "don't run," a 2-year-old's brain registers "run." The negative word triggers the behavior you were trying to stop. This is not defiance. This is neurology — their prefrontal cortex is still under construction.

Once I understood this, I started replacing commands with instructions:

- "Don't touch the TV" → "Hands off, please. The TV is for looking at."
- "Stop climbing on that" → "Feet on the floor. The climber is for climbing."
- "We have to leave the playground now" → "First we go down the slide one more time. Then we walk to the car together."

Three parts to every redirection: what you want them to do, the reason (brief), and what they can do instead.

I also learned that the biggest variable in how a toddler responds is the parent's internal state. If I was running on empty, my corrections came out sharp. My kid escalated. We both ended up upset.

The fix was not becoming a calm parent. The fix was the five-second pause before responding. Not to be serene. Just to break the automatic reaction cycle and ask: what does my kid actually need right now?

Sometimes the answer is a boundary. Sometimes it is food. Sometimes it is just attention.

I put together a full guide with the phrase system and the environmental setup that reduced power struggles in our house by about 70%. It is free to download — there is a checklist in the guide that I printed and stuck on my fridge.

Happy to answer questions if anything is unclear.
