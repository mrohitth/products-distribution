# No Lease. Deposit Back.

## The 50-State Guide to Recovering Your Security Deposit When There's No Written Agreement

---

## Introduction: The Paperwork Problem That Isn't Your Problem

You paid your deposit. You moved in. And somewhere along the way, nothing was in writing—maybe your landlord said "we'll sort it out," maybe you assumed a handshake was enough, maybe the previous tenant dealt with your landlord directly and no one thought to make it formal. Now you're facing a move-out, and you're hearing from your landlord or building manager that the deposit is "complicated," that there "was no agreement," that you should "check with the previous person."

Let me be direct: you're being misled.

Not necessarily maliciously—some landlords genuinely don't understand the law. But the law doesn't care whether you had paperwork. In every single state, security deposits create legal obligations whether or not there's a written lease. The money you paid isn't a gift. It wasn't a bonus. It was a deposit, and your landlord knows it.

The problem is those rights are buried in your state's legal code, they vary wildly from state to state, and the whole system is designed to make you give up before you get what you owe.

This guide is what you reach for when the deposit you earned back is being withheld over a paperwork technicality. It walks you through exactly what your rights are when there's no written lease, exactly what you can demand, and exactly where you file if your landlord won't cooperate—with timelines, templates, and decision trees for all 50 states.

You will leave this guide knowing your number, knowing your deadline, and knowing exactly what to send your landlord today.

Here's how we'll get you there.

## Part I: Knowing Your Ground Before You Start

Before you send a single letter or file a single form, you need to know what you're working with. Most tenants who lose their deposits do so because they don't understand the value of their claim or the strength of their legal position. This section fixes that.

### Chapter 1: Which State Law Controls Your Case (And Why Oral Agreements Still Create Rights)

Every state treats security deposits differently. California's rules are nothing like Texas's. New York's approach bears no resemblance to Florida's. And the rules that apply to you are determined by one thing and one thing only: where you live.

**Why Location Is Everything**

Security deposit law is almost entirely a creature of state statute. That means your rights, your timeline, and your remedies are written into your state's legal code—not federal law, not the Constitution, not "common sense." The first step in recovering your deposit is identifying which statute applies to your situation.

Most states have a specific section of their landlord-tenant code that addresses security deposits. These sections typically cover:

- Maximum deposit amounts landlords can collect
- Where deposits must be held (interest-bearing accounts, separate accounts)
- Required move-out timelines for returning deposits
- Itemization requirements for deductions
- Penalties for landlords who violate the rules

Here's the critical point: most of these statutes apply by default to any rental arrangement, regardless of whether there's a written lease. The law creates the landlord-tenant relationship; the lease just documents it. If you paid money to move into a unit and live there, you're a tenant. Full stop.

**Finding Your State's Deposit Statute**

Your state's security deposit law will typically be found in one of these locations:

- A dedicated "Security Deposit" section within the landlord-tenant code
- The "Landlord's Obligations" chapter of your state's property code
- The "Residential Tenancies" or "Landlord and Tenant Act" for your state

Search your state name plus "security deposit statute" or "security deposit limit" to find the relevant code section. Look specifically for:

- **The deposit cap**: Many states limit deposits to one or two months' rent. If you paid more than this cap, your landlord may owe you the excess regardless.
- **The return timeline**: This is the deadline your landlord must meet when returning your deposit after move-out. It ranges from 14 days (Alabama, Alaska) to 60 days (Washington).
- **The penalty clause**: Most states automatically penalize landlords who miss the deadline—often by doubling or tripling the deposit amount. This is free money for you, and it applies automatically. You don't have to ask for it.

**The Oral Agreement Reality**

I know what you're thinking: "This all sounds great, but I don't have proof I paid a deposit."

Here's the thing: you almost certainly do. We'll get into the details in Chapter 3, but for now understand this—most states recognize implied contracts. If you paid money to move into a rental unit, an implied security deposit arrangement exists whether or not anyone wrote it down. Your landlord can't simply claim "there was no deposit" when they have your money.

Several legal theories protect you here:

- **Implied contract**: The circumstances of your tenancy suggest a deposit was intended, even without explicit agreement.
- **Unjust enrichment**: Your landlord received a benefit (your money) at your expense, and it's unfair for them to keep it without providing what was understood in exchange.
- **Statutory protection**: Even if no agreement existed (it does), most states' deposit statutes apply automatically to any landlord receiving a deposit from any tenant.

Your landlord cannot escape their legal obligations by pointing to the absence of a piece of paper they were responsible for creating.

**Action Steps for Today:**

1. Search for your state + "security deposit statute" to find your state's specific law.
2. Locate the section on maximum deposit limits and note your state's cap.
3. Find the section on return timelines and note your deadline.
4. Find the section on penalties and note what your landlord owes if they miss the deadline.

### Chapter 2: Calculate Your Actual Deposit Exposure

You need to know your number before you can fight for it. "My landlord owes me my deposit" is vague. "My landlord owes me $2,400, plus $2,400 in statutory penalties, for a total of $4,800" is a claim that gets attention.

**What Counts as a Security Deposit**

Security deposits aren't always labeled "security deposit" on your receipt or bank statement. They can appear in various forms:

- A traditional security deposit (first month's rent + security)
- A "last month's rent" payment at move-in
- A "pet deposit" or "animal deposit"
- A transfer from a previous landlord to a new one
- An "amenity deposit" or "key deposit" (these sometimes qualify)
- Installment payments toward a deposit over time

If you paid any amount to move into your rental that wasn't explicitly labeled as rent for a specific month, document it. The full total is what you're entitled to get back.

**State Maximums and What They Mean**

Many states cap security deposits at one or two months' rent. If you live in one of these states and paid more than the legal maximum, you may be entitled to immediate return of the excess—even before move-out.

Examples of state deposit caps:

- **California**: Maximum two months' rent (unfurnished) or three months' (furnished)
- **New York**: No state cap, but NYC has its own rules (up to one month's rent for most apartments)
- **Texas**: No state cap, but landlords must return deposits within 30 days
- **Florida**: Maximum two months' rent if rent is under $2,000/month; three months if higher
- **Washington**: Maximum one month's rent (or 1.5x monthly rent for deposits paid in installments)

Check your state's cap and compare it to what you actually paid. If you overpaid, that's additional leverage.

**Building Your Deposit Ledger**

Create a simple document showing:

1. Every payment you made at move-in with dates and amounts
2. Any portion of those payments that was explicitly rent vs. deposit
3. Any additional deposits paid during your tenancy (pet deposits, vehicle deposits, etc.)
4. Your state's maximum deposit limit
5. Your total qualifying deposit amount

This ledger becomes your baseline number. Everything else—penalties, interest, damages disputes—builds on top of it.

**Action Steps for Today:**

1. Pull all your bank statements or payment records from your move-in period.
2. List every payment you made with dates and amounts.
3. Identify which payments were rent (for a specific month) vs. deposit.
4. Calculate your total qualifying deposit amount.
5. Check your state's maximum deposit limit and note if you overpaid.

### Chapter 3: Document the Undocumented—What Evidence You Already Have

You don't need a lease to prove a deposit existed. You need evidence. And almost certainly, you already have more than you realize.

**The Evidence Hierarchy**

Not all evidence is equal. Courts weight different types of documentation based on reliability and contemporaneous nature. Here's what you're looking for, from strongest to weakest:

**Tier 1: Direct Financial Records**

- Bank statements showing transfers or payments to your landlord
- Venmo, Zelle, Cash App, or PayPal transaction records
- Canceled checks
- Money order or cashier's check receipts
- Credit card statements

These records prove money changed hands. That's the foundation of your claim.

**Tier 2: Digital Communication**

- Text messages with your landlord discussing the deposit or move-in
- Emails referencing deposit amounts, move-in conditions, or the arrangement
- Voicemails (transcribe them and save the audio file)
- In-app messages through rental platforms (even if you found the place elsewhere)
- Social media messages if your landlord communicated through those channels

**Tier 3: Witnesses and Third-Party Records**

- Statements from roommates who were present during negotiations
- Correspondence with the previous tenant about the deposit transfer
- Property management company records (if the building uses one)
- Utility accounts showing move-in dates
- Renter's insurance applications (often ask for deposit amount)
- Photos or videos of move-in conditions with timestamps

**Tier 4: Circumstantial Evidence**

- Your own contemporaneous notes (if dated)
- Calendar entries
- Personal records of any kind showing consistent references to the deposit

**Building Your Evidence File**

Organize everything chronologically in a dedicated folder—physical or digital. Include:

- A timeline narrative explaining the sequence of events
- All financial records with relevant transactions highlighted
- All communications with annotations
- Any photos or videos with dates verified
- Names and contact information for any potential witnesses

You don't need perfect evidence. You need enough to establish that a deposit was paid and that your landlord knows it. In small claims court, you're often appearing before a judge who understands that most tenants don't keep perfect records—and the judge will work with what you have.

**Action Steps for Today:**

1. Download your bank statements for the period around your move-in.
2. Search your phone for any text conversations with your landlord.
3. Search your email for any messages about the deposit or move-in.
4. Create a folder and start organizing what you've found.
5. Contact any roommates or witnesses and let them know you may need a statement later.

**The Itemization Requirement**

Most states require your landlord to provide a written, itemized statement of any deductions from your deposit—typically within the same deadline as the return itself. Common valid deductions include:

- Unpaid rent
- Damage beyond normal wear and tear
- Cleaning costs (if required beyond normal move-out cleaning)

What's NOT a valid deduction:

- Normal wear and tear (a slightly worn carpet, faded paint, minor scuffs)
- Routine cleaning if the unit was clean when you moved in
- Improvements you made during your tenancy that the landlord didn't request
- Any amount beyond your actual deposit (landlords can't "charge" you for things they didn't cover with your deposit)

**The Penalty Is Automatic**

This bears repeating: in most states with penalty provisions, the statutory penalty applies automatically when your landlord misses the deadline. You don't have to file a lawsuit to trigger it. Your landlord doesn't get to "make it right" later and avoid the penalty. The minute the deadline passed without returning your deposit, your landlord owed you double or triple the original amount.

This is your leverage. Use it.

5. Add up your deposit amount and your penalty exposure to get your current claim value.

### Chapter 5: Finding the Statute That Protects You When There's No Written Agreement

Your state's deposit statute is your primary weapon. Let's learn how to read it.

**Locating Your Statute**

Search for your state in this format: "[State] security deposit statute [state code number]"

For example: "California security deposit statute Civil Code 1950.5" or "New York security deposit statute General Obligations Law 7-103"

Many states have helpful annotated codes that include brief explanations of what each section means. FindLaw, Justia, and the Legal Information Institute (Cornell) all offer free access to state codes with plain-language notes.

**Identifying the Key Language**

When you find your statute, focus on these sections:

1. **Definitions**: What counts as a security deposit under this law?
2. **Landlord obligations**: What must the landlord do with the deposit?
3. **Return requirements**: When and how must the landlord return it?
4. **Penalty provisions**: What happens when the landlord violates the law?
5. **Tenant remedies**: What can you do when the landlord violates?

Look specifically for language that addresses verbal or informal agreements. Many states have statutes that apply by default to any landlord-tenant arrangement. Look for terms like:

- "Regardless of the terms of any rental agreement"
- "Any security deposit received"
- "When a landlord receives a security deposit"
- "The provisions of this section apply to all residential rental agreements"

If your statute includes language like this, your landlord's failure to document your agreement doesn't matter. The statute fills in the gaps.

**Three Legal Theories, One Goal**

Even if your statute doesn't explicitly address verbal agreements (most do by implication), you typically have three overlapping legal theories to pursue:

1. **Statutory claim**: Your state's deposit statute creates an obligation. You don't need a separate agreement for it to apply.
2. **Implied contract**: Given the circumstances—payment of money, moving into the unit, expectation of its return—an implied deposit agreement exists.
3. **Unjust enrichment**: Your landlord received a benefit (your deposit) at your expense, and allowing them to keep it would be fundamentally unfair.

You don't need all three. You just need one. But having multiple theories strengthens your position and gives a judge multiple reasons to rule in your favor.

**Action Steps for Today:**

1. Locate your state's current security deposit statute online.
2. Read the definitions section and note how your state defines "security deposit."
3. Identify the section on landlord obligations and note what your landlord was supposed to do.
4. Find the penalty section and confirm your penalty exposure.
5. Look for language indicating these provisions apply regardless of lease formality.

### Chapter 6: Your Small Claims Court Jurisdiction and Filing Fee

If your landlord won't return your deposit voluntarily, small claims court is where you go. And here's the good news: small claims court was designed for people like you.

**Understanding Small Claims Court**

Small claims court is a streamlined legal process for disputes under a certain dollar amount. There are no lawyers required. Rules of evidence are relaxed. Proceedings are faster than regular civil court. And in most states, the filing fees are reasonable (typically $30-$150).

The maximum claim amount varies by state:

- **$2,500 limit**: Alabama, Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Delaware, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Maine, Maryland, Minnesota, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Vermont, Washington, West Virginia, Wisconsin, Wyoming
- **$5,000 limit**: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Florida, Illinois, Indiana, Louisiana, Massachusetts, Michigan, Mississippi, New York, North Carolina, Texas, Virginia
- **$7,500 limit**: New Mexico (if filed in magistrate court)
- **$10,000 limit**: Georgia (if plaintiff elects), Indiana (if represented by attorney)

**Where to File**

You file your claim in the county where the rental property is located. This is typically called "venue" in legal terms. If you moved out of state and your landlord is in another state, you generally file in the county where the property sits, not where you currently live.

**Filing Fees**

Most filing fees fall between $30 and $150, depending on the claim amount and state. Some states offer fee waivers for low-income filers. Contact your local small claims court for exact fees.

**What You'll Need to File**

When you file your small claims complaint, you'll typically need:

1. A completed complaint form (available at the courthouse or online)
2. Your evidence file (organized chronologically)
3. The filing fee
4. The defendant's name and address (your landlord or property manager)
5. The amount you're claiming (deposit + statutory penalty, if applicable)

**After You File**

Once filed, the court will schedule a hearing date and serve your landlord with a copy of your claim. Your landlord may:

- Pay you before the hearing (this happens more often than you'd expect)
- Respond with their own claims or defenses
- Show up to dispute the matter

Either way, you're now in a formal legal process, and your landlord is on notice.

**Action Steps for Today:**

1. Look up the small claims court limit in your state.
2. Confirm your claim amount (deposit + penalties) doesn't exceed the limit.
3. Find the small claims court address for the county where you rented.
4. Check the filing fee for your claim amount in your county.
5. Download or request a copy of the complaint form for your state.

### Chapter 7: The Demand Letter—Your First Shot Across the Bow

Before you file anything, you send a demand letter. This serves two purposes: it puts your landlord on official notice, and it gives them a chance to resolve this without further action. Most landlords would rather pay what they owe than deal with a lawsuit.

**What a Demand Letter Does**

A demand letter is a formal written request for the return of your security deposit, citing the specific law your landlord has violated. It's not a threat—it's a notice. But it signals that you know the law, you know your rights, and you're prepared to pursue them.

**What to Include**

Your demand letter should contain:

1. **Your name and address**: Who you are and where you rented.
2. **The deposit amount**: Exact dollar figure you paid, with documentation reference.
3. **The move-out date**: When your tenancy ended.
4. **The statutory deadline**: Your state's required return timeline and the date it passed.
5. **The amount demanded**: Deposit plus any statutory penalty.
6. **The legal basis**: A citation to your state's security deposit statute.
7. **A deadline for response**: Typically 10-14 days from the letter's date.
8. **Consequences of non-response**: That you will file in small claims court.
9. **Your signature**: Typed name with contact information.

**Sample Demand Letter Structure**

*[Your Name]*
*[Your Current Address]*
*[City, State ZIP]*
*[Phone Number]*
*[Email Address]*

*[Date]*

*[Landlord Name]*
*[Landlord Address]*
*[City, State ZIP]*

**Re: Demand for Return of Security Deposit — [Property Address]**

Dear [Landlord Name],

I am writing to demand the return of my security deposit in the amount of $[X], plus statutory penalties in the amount of $[Y], for a total demand of $[Z]. This amount is owed pursuant to [State] [Code Section], which requires landlords to return security deposits within [number] days of the termination of tenancy.

I moved out of [property address] on [date], and [number] days have elapsed since that date. As of today, I have not received my deposit or an itemized statement of deductions.

I paid a security deposit of $[amount] on or about [date], as documented in [bank records/text messages/other evidence]. Under [State] law, the failure to return this deposit within the statutory period triggers [penalty provision].

I demand payment of $[total amount] within 14 days of this letter. If I do not receive payment by [date], I will file a claim in [County] Small Claims Court, where I am prepared to pursue the full amount owed, including statutory penalties and court costs.

Please send payment to the address listed above or contact me to arrange payment.

Sincerely,

*[Your signature]*
*[Your printed name]*

**Sending the Letter**

Send your demand letter by:

- **Certified mail with return receipt**: This creates a record of delivery and is the gold standard for legal correspondence.
- **Email plus certified mail**: Send the same letter by email for speed, followed by certified mail for legal weight.
- **Keep a copy**: Always keep a copy of every letter you send.

**Action Steps for Today:**

1. Draft your demand letter using the structure above, adapted to your state and situation.
2. Calculate your total demand (deposit + statutory penalty).
3. Date the letter for today.
4. Send it by certified mail with return receipt.
5. Keep a copy for your records.

### Chapter 8: Negotiation and Settlement—When Your Landlord Responds

Your demand letter worked—or at least got a response. Now you're negotiating. This is where knowing your law matters, because your landlord or their attorney will try to lowball you or invent reasons to keep your money.

**Common Landlord Responses and How to Handle Them**

**"We never received your deposit."**

This is the most common lie. Your response: "Per [State] statute, landlords who receive deposits are required to maintain records of them. My bank records show a transfer of $[amount] to [account/name] on [date]. Please provide your records showing the deposit was not received."

**"You owe for damages."**

Deductions for damages are valid—but only if properly documented. Your response: "Under [State] law, you are required to provide an itemized statement of deductions within [timeframe]. No such statement was provided. Please provide the itemized statement and supporting documentation (photos, estimates) within 10 days."

**"The previous tenant was supposed to handle this."**

This isn't your problem. Your response: "I am the tenant who paid this deposit. My payment records show $[amount] transferred on [date]. The arrangement between you and the previous tenant is between you and them."

**"We'll pay you back next month / when we sell the property / eventually."**

Don't accept promises. Your response: "The deposit is owed now under [State] law. I am willing to negotiate a payment plan with a signed agreement and specific dates, but I will not agree to indefinite postponement."

**Negotiation Tips**

- **Know your floor**: Your deposit is yours. Any offer below your deposit amount is a loss. Statutory penalties are bonus—don't give them up unless the landlord offers real money quickly.
- **Get everything in writing**: Any settlement agreement should be signed and in writing before you accept payment.
- **Timing matters**: Landlords are often more motivated to settle right before a hearing than they are now. Don't rush to settle at the first offer.
- **Consider your costs**: If you're asking for $1,200 and your filing fee is $50, settling for $900 might make sense if it saves you time. Do the math.

**When to Accept Settlement**

Reasonable settlement scenarios:

- Full deposit plus some or all statutory penalties
- Full deposit paid immediately
- Deposit plus reasonable deductions for documented damages (and only with itemized proof)

Unreasonable settlement scenarios:

- "We'll give you $200 and call it even"
- "Sign this release saying you'll never contact us again for any reason"
- "Pay us $300 for 'cleaning costs' and we'll call it square"

**Action Steps for Today:**

1. If you received a response to your demand letter, read it carefully and identify their stated reason for withholding the deposit.
2. Prepare a written response addressing their specific claim.
3. Calculate the minimum you'll accept (your deposit amount) and your ideal outcome (deposit plus penalties).
4. Keep all correspondence in writing from this point forward.
5. Set a reasonable deadline for response before escalating to filing.

### Chapter 9: Filing Your Small Claims Case

If negotiation fails, you file. Small claims court is accessible, affordable, and effective—and you don't need a lawyer to win.

**Preparing Your Case File**

Organize your evidence into a clear, logical presentation:

1. **The Deposit Itself**: Bank records showing the payment, with amounts and dates clearly marked.
2. **The Tenancy**: Evidence you lived in the unit (utility bills, mail, photos, lease if any).
3. **Move-Out Documentation**: Move-out date, any forwarding address provided, any communication about the deposit.
4. **The Landlord's Failure**: Timeline showing the deadline passed without return of deposit or proper itemization.
5. **Your Demand**: Copy of the demand letter and proof of delivery (certified mail receipt).

**Filing the Complaint**

Go to the courthouse or file online (many states now allow electronic filing). Submit:

1. Your completed complaint form (check the court's website for the specific form)
2. Your filing fee
3. Your evidence (copies for the court and the defendant)

The court will:

1. Assign a case number
2. Schedule a hearing date (typically 4-8 weeks out)
3. Arrange for the defendant to be served (sometimes you do this yourself)

**Serving the Landlord**

Your landlord must be formally served with a copy of your complaint and notice of the hearing. Methods vary by state but typically include:

- Personal service (someone hands them the documents)
- Certified mail with acknowledgment of receipt
- Service by sheriff or process server

Keep proof of service—your hearing cannot proceed without it.

**Presenting Your Case**

At the hearing:

- Be early and dressed appropriately (not a suit, but neat)
- Bring original documents AND copies (one for the judge, one for the defendant)
- Present your evidence in chronological order
- Keep your presentation simple and clear
- Answer the judge's questions directly

Your landlord may or may not appear. If they don't, you typically win by default. If they do appear, they may:

- Acknowledge the debt and negotiate on the spot
- Present their own evidence and arguments
- Argue that you owe them money for damages

If your landlord counterclaims (claims you owe them), you have the right to respond. Bring your move-in photos if you have them, and be ready to discuss the condition of the unit.

**After You Win**

If the judge rules in your favor, you'll receive a judgment. Collecting on that judgment is sometimes the hard part. Options include:

- Landlord pays voluntarily (happens more often than you'd expect once there's a judgment)
- Writ of execution (court order to seize assets)
- Garnishment (taking money from bank accounts or wages)
- Mechanic's lien on the property (in some states)

**Action Steps for Today:**

1. Organize your evidence file in chronological order.
2. Download or obtain your state's small claims complaint form.
3. Contact the courthouse to confirm filing procedures and fees.
4. Decide whether to file in person or online.
5. Prepare your check or payment for the filing fee.

### Chapter 11: Your Deposit Protection Toolkit for Your Next Rental

Don't let this happen again. Here's how to protect yourself in every future rental:

**Before You Move In**

1. **Get a written receipt for every payment**, no matter how small. "Received $1,200 security deposit from [name] for [address]" with a signature and date is enough.
2. **Write your own lease summary** if no formal lease exists. Two sentences: "Security deposit of $[amount] paid on [date]. Deposit to be returned within [state deadline] of move-out minus lawful deductions." Have your landlord initial it.
3. **Take photos and videos** of every room on move-in day. Date-stamp them. Store them somewhere permanent (cloud, email to yourself).
4. **Use payment methods that create records**: Bank transfers, checks, or payment apps are better than cash. If you pay cash, get a signed receipt every time.
5. **Confirm the deposit account** in states where landlords are required to hold deposits separately. Ask: "Where is my deposit being held?"

**During Your Tenancy**

1. **Keep all payment records** in one place. Bank statements, receipts, screenshots.
2. **Document everything in writing**—maintenance requests, agreed-upon repairs, any conversations about the deposit.
3. **Check your state's laws** annually. Rules change.

**When You Move Out**

1. **Send move-out notice in writing** (required in most states, even without a lease). Email plus certified mail.
2. **Provide your forwarding address in writing**. Landlords can't be penalized for failing to return deposits if they don't know where to send them.
3. **Document the move-out condition**. Video walkthrough with timestamps.
4. **Follow up in writing** if you don't hear within the statutory period. Send another letter.
5. **Track your deadline** on a calendar. Start your demand letter the day after it passes.

**A Simple Deposit Protection Checklist**

Before any rental arrangement:

- [ ] Written receipt for deposit payment (email or paper)
- [ ] Photos/videos of move-in condition with date stamp
- [ ] Payment method that creates a record
- [ ] Move-in date documented (utilities, mail, etc.)
- [ ] Landlord's contact information confirmed in writing

After move-out:

- [ ] Written move-out notice sent
- [ ] Forwarding address provided in writing
- [ ] Move-out condition documented
- [ ] Calendar set for state deadline
- [ ] Demand letter drafted and ready to send
