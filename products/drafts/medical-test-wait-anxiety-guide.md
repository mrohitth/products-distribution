# "What If It's Bad": A Field Guide to Waiting

---

## Introduction

You know the drill. You've been here before or you're here now: a call back, a scan, a biopsy scheduled, a referral waiting. Your brain has already written the whole thing. The diagnosis, the treatment, the unraveling. You can see the tubes. You can feel the blood pressure cuff. You rehearsed the conversation with your family.

And here's what nobody tells you: this is not weakness. This is what a brilliant, threat-detecting nervous system does when it can't find enough data. It fills the silence with worst-case scenarios. It's not trying to hurt you. It's trying to protect you.

But the protection isn't working, is it?

What you need are tools that work with your nervous system, not against it. Not affirmations. Not "just think positive." Real, nervous-system-aware strategies that pull you back to now when your mind has gone six months ahead.

This guide is built for that.

In these pages, you'll learn how to interrupt the catastrophic loop before it hijacks you, ground yourself in your actual body when your brain has gone to the ICU, and build a personal toolkit you can actually deploy at 2am when your hands won't stop shaking. You will not think your way out of this. But you can regulate your way through it.

This is not about being positive. It's about being here — in your actual life, in your actual body — while you wait for information that hasn't arrived yet.

Let's get you through this.

# Part 1: The Body First — Calming the Alarm System

## Why Your Body Has to Go First

Here's something that will feel counterintuitive: the reason you can't stop the catastrophic thoughts is not a thinking problem. It's a body problem.

Your nervous system is running threat-assessment protocols right now. It detected uncertainty, and uncertainty is dangerous from an evolutionary standpoint. So your body shifted into a mode designed for immediate physical threats — increased heart rate, shallow breathing, hypervigilance, racing thoughts. The problem is, there's no lion to run from. The threat is hypothetical. But your nervous system doesn't know that. It just knows something is wrong.

When your body is in this state, your prefrontal cortex — the part of your brain responsible for rational thinking, probability assessment, and executive function — goes partially offline. It's not broken. It's just deprioritized. Your nervous system is routing resources toward survival, not toward your ability to Google medical statistics at 3am.

This is why "just don't think about it" doesn't work. You can't think your way out of a body state. But you can regulate your way out of it.

The strategies in this part are all about switching on the body's calming mechanisms. Not suppressing the alarm — just creating enough safety signal that the alarm doesn't have to run the whole show.

## Chapter 1: The 4-4-4 Breathing Reset

You've probably heard of "box breathing." Maybe you've tried it and thought it felt cheesy or ineffective. Here's why that happens: most people try to breathe their way calm while their nervous system is still screaming "DANGER" at full volume. Breathing alone can't override a full alarm state. But it can start to signal safety — if you do it right.

The 4-4-4 pattern (four counts in, hold for four, four counts out) works because the extended exhale activates your parasympathetic nervous system, the part of your autonomic system that says "we can rest now." But the magic isn't just the pattern. It's the counting.

Counting is a tactile anchor. When you count, your brain has to track something real and immediate. You can't count and catastrophize at the same time. The counting creates a small gap — a neural break — between the trigger (the scary thought) and the reaction (the full panic spiral). That gap is everything.

### How to do it right:

Find a seat. Plant your feet on the floor. Feel the ground under you. You're not trying to relax — you're trying to regulate. There's a difference.

Breathe in through your nose for four counts. Don't gulp. Just a steady, medium breath.

Hold for four counts. If four is too long, do three. The point is the hold, not the number. A brief hold interrupts the cycle.

Exhale through your mouth for four counts. Let it out slower than it went in. The slow exhale is where the calming signal happens.

Repeat four times. Not ten. Not twenty. Four. You're building a neural pathway, not running a marathon.

**The key instruction:** When your brain wanders to the worst-case scenario mid-breath — and it will — just notice. "I'm off counting. That's okay. Back to four." Don't judge. Don't narrate. Just redirect. You're not failing; you're practicing.

### When to use it:

- Before you check your phone for results
- At 2am when you wake up and the thoughts start
- In the waiting room before your name is called
- Before you open your laptop to Google symptoms

The goal is to make this automatic. Practice it twice a day when you're not panicking, so your body knows the pattern when you need it. It should feel slightly boring. That's how you know it's working.

## Chapter 2: Mapping Your 5-4-3-2-1 in Under 90 Seconds

The 5-4-3-2-1 grounding technique is well-known, but most people do it wrong. They try to complete the full sequence in order every time, which takes effort and attention they don't have when they're spiraling. What you actually need is a rapid-interrupt tool — something you can deploy in 60 to 90 seconds that yanks you back to present reality before the loop accelerates.

Here's how to do it for speed:

### The Rapid Version:

Start with **5 things you can see.** Name them out loud if you can. The chair. The door. The pattern on the ceiling. The crack in the wall. Your own hands. Speaking grounds you more than thinking silently — use your voice if the space allows.

**4 things you can touch.** Press your feet into the floor. Feel the texture of your jeans. Run your hand along the armrest. The sensation of temperature, texture, weight — these pull your nervous system out of the hypothetical and into the actual.

**3 things you can hear.** The hum of the refrigerator. Traffic outside. Your own breathing. Someone's footsteps in the hallway. Name them. The act of labeling pulls your verbal brain online, which competes with the panic loop.

**2 things you can smell.** This is the hardest one because most environments don't have strong smells. Keep it simple. The soap in the bathroom. The coffee on your desk. If there's nothing, move to the next step — don't let the absence become a new source of anxiety.

**1 thing you can taste.** The lingering flavor of your last drink. The inside of your cheek. The texture of your tongue against your teeth. Something. Anything.

That's it. You just did a full grounding sequence in under 90 seconds. You didn't have to feel better. You just had to come back to the room.

### The tactical version:

In a waiting room: Look at the floor tiles. Count them. Feel your back against the chair. Notice the temperature. Listen for one voice. That's enough.

In your car: Hands on the steering wheel. Feel the texture. See the dashboard. Hear the engine. That's three steps done.

In bed at 2am: Feel the sheet. See the shape of your body under the blanket. Hear one sound. Don't complete the whole sequence — even two or three steps is a win.

### The important thing:

The goal is not to feel peaceful. It's to feel present. You might still feel scared. You might still feel awful. But you are here — in this room, on this floor, in this body — instead of in a hypothetical ICU that doesn't exist yet.

That's the win.

## Chapter 3: The Physiological Sigh That Quiets the Storm

There's a reason you sometimes catch yourself sighing without meaning to. It's not weakness or frustration — it's your body running a self-calibration program. Double-inhale sighs are one of the most rapid ways to reduce acute stress, and they're backed by research from Huberman Lab and others studying the neuroscience of breath.

The mechanism is simple: when you exhale sharply through your mouth, you pop open alveoli in your lungs that have partially collapsed. Collapsed alveoli mean reduced gas exchange, which your body reads as "not enough air," which triggers a low-grade panic signal. When you pop them open with a big sigh, your body gets the message: we're actually okay on oxygen. The alarm volume drops.

### How to do it:

You don't need to do this perfectly. You just need to understand the mechanics.

**Step 1: Take a full breath in through your nose.** Not a shallow one. Fill your lungs.

**Step 2: Take a second, shorter inhale through your nose.** Top off the breath. You should feel your chest and upper abdomen expand more than they did on the first inhale.

**Step 3: Exhale sharply through your mouth.**全部放出去 — let it all out. Don't control it. Let the exhale be louder than the inhale.

That's one physiological sigh. Do two in a row.

When you do this, pay attention to what happens. Most people report a noticeable drop in the subjective feeling of panic — not calm, exactly, but a kind of cellular exhale. The screaming part of your nervous system quiets by a few notches.

### When to use it:

- Right before you pick up the phone to call the doctor's office
- When you feel your chest tightening in the middle of the day
- Before you open the patient portal
- When you've been in a spin for more than 20 minutes and can't break it

### A note on "belly breathing" vs. chest breathing:

You might have heard that deep breathing should come from your belly. For the physiological sigh, it doesn't matter. You're maximizing lung capacity, not practicing perfect form. What matters is that you fill up. Let your belly expand. Let your chest expand. The inhale is from the bottom up.

The exhale is where the work happens. The sharp, full exhale through your mouth — that's what signals safety to your nervous system. Do it like you're fogging a mirror.

---

**Key Takeaway:** Your body is the entry point, not your thoughts. When panic is running, you cannot outthink it — but you can out-breathe it. Practice the 4-4-4, the rapid 5-4-3-2-1, and the physiological sigh so they become automatic. You need them at 2am when you can't sleep, not after you've read instructions.

# Part 2: Naming the Beast — Understanding Why You Spiral

## Why Knowing the Mechanism Helps

Here's something that will feel strange but is actually important: understanding why you catastrophize doesn't make you stop catastrophizing. But it takes away the mystery, and the mystery is part of what makes it feel so overwhelming.

When you don't know why you're doing this — when it feels like you're losing your mind, like something is fundamentally wrong with you, like you're the only person who can't just "handle" waiting — the spiral has fuel. The shame and confusion stack on top of the fear.

But when you understand that your brain is running a completely predictable, evolutionary defensible threat-assessment protocol, something shifts. The thoughts don't become less scary. But you stop believing that the thoughts mean something terrible about you.

This section is about giving you the map. Once you know why your brain does this, you can catch yourself doing it. And catching yourself is the first step to interrupting it.

---

## Chapter 4: The "Pre-Grieving" Trap — Why Your Brain Rehearses the Worst

There's a term for what's happening when you're mentally walking through a worst-case diagnosis, imagining the treatment, picturing the aftermath: it's called pre-grieving.

You might not have heard that term before, but you've done it. You know you've done it. At some point in the last few days — maybe right now — you let yourself rehearse a terrible outcome. You saw it. You felt it. You grieved something that hasn't happened yet.

Your brain does this as a threat-assessment strategy. It's trying to prepare you. To "get ahead" of the pain. To build up some kind of immunity to bad news by running through it in advance. Your nervous system thinks this is helpful. It's not.

The problem with pre-grieving is that it doesn't prepare you — it traumatizes you in advance. You're doing the emotional work of a worst-case scenario without any of the information that would make that work meaningful. You're grieving something that might not happen, and meanwhile, you're exhausting yourself. You wake up tired. You can't focus. You feel like you're already falling apart, and the news hasn't even arrived yet.

### How to catch yourself pre-grieving:

The first step is recognition. Most people don't have language for this — they just know they feel terrible and they don't know why. So here's the language:

**Pre-grieving is any mental rehearsal of a worst-case scenario that includes emotional investment.** If you're imagining the diagnosis and feeling your chest tighten, feeling your breath get shallow, feeling the weight of it — that's pre-grieving. If you're mentally practicing how you'd tell your family, how you'd adjust your life, what the treatment would be like — that's pre-grieving.

Noticing is not judging. You are not weak or broken for doing this. Your brain is doing what brains do.

### How to interrupt the pre-grieving loop:

Once you catch yourself, try this:

**Step 1: Name it.** Say it out loud or in your head: "I'm pre-grieving right now." Naming it creates separation. It's a thought about a thought, not the thought itself.

**Step 2: Ask the question.** "Is this happening right now?" The answer is no. It might happen. But it's not happening now. Your nervous system is treating a hypothetical like a present emergency. You're not in the hospital. You're on your couch. Or in your car. Or at work. You're here.

**Step 3: Come back to your body.** Use a quick grounding technique — one breath, one sensation, whatever you can manage. The goal is not to feel fine. It's to stop the rehearsal.

### The hard truth:

You cannot prevent a worst-case outcome by rehearsing it. You can't control what happens medically by thinking about it. All you can do is exhaust yourself before you get there.

That's not fatalism. It's just physics. Emotional energy is finite. You get to choose where it goes.

## Chapter 5: Probability Distortion — Why 1% Feels Like 100%

Here's something your brain does that sounds insane when you see it written out: it treats the worst-case scenario as if it's the most likely scenario.

When you're waiting for test results, your brain has access to a tiny dataset: you have a symptom, you had a test, something might be wrong. Based on that tiny dataset, your brain is constructing a probability estimate. And because negative outcomes feel more dangerous than positive outcomes feel safe — due to something called "negativity bias" — your brain massively overweights the bad possibilities.

In reality, most tests come back fine. Most symptoms are benign. Most referrals are routine. But you don't feel that. You feel the 1% as if it's 100%. Because your nervous system doesn't care about base rates. It cares about threat detection.

This is probability distortion.

Here's how it plays out: You have a 3% chance of a serious diagnosis based on your symptom profile. But when you close your eyes, it feels like 80%. You feel the weight of it. You see it. You can almost touch it. The number 3% exists somewhere in the back of your head, but it's silent. Meanwhile, the imagined scenario is loud and vivid and feels real.

Why? Because your brain evolved to prioritize vivid threats. In the savanna, the rustle in the grass that might be a lion deserves more attention than the absence of rustling. Your prehistoric ancestors survived by assuming the worst — and that strategy got hardwired into your nervous system. The problem is, you don't live on the savanna anymore. And the rustle in the grass is usually just the wind.

### How to correct the distortion:

**Step 1: Get the actual number.** Ask your doctor, if you can: "Based on my symptom profile, how often does this turn out to be something serious? Roughly, in your experience?" Don't let the answer scare you. Just get the number. Numbers are boring — and boring is the opposite of panic.

**Step 2: Write it down.** When your brain tells you "this is almost certainly bad," write down the actual number next to it. "My brain feels like it's 90% bad. The doctor said it's about 5% bad." Seeing the gap between felt probability and actual probability is jarring — in a good way. It shows you how much your nervous system is distorting.

**Step 3: Use the gap as an anchor.** When you notice the gap — when you feel "it's almost certainly bad" but you know the real number is lower — that's the moment to pull back. You don't have to argue with the feeling. You just have to notice it. "My brain is distorting again. The number is lower than this feels."

This won't make you feel calm. It will make you feel informed. There's a difference. Informed is enough.

## Chapter 6: The "Safety Behaviors" That Backfire

When you're waiting for medical results, you do things that feel like they help but actually make things worse. These are called safety behaviors, and they're one of the most insidious parts of health anxiety.

Safety behaviors are actions you take to reduce uncertainty or prepare for the worst. They feel protective. They feel reasonable. And they almost always backfire.

### Common safety behaviors:

**Googling symptoms.** You've done it. You open a browser at 11pm, type in your symptom, and immediately land on a page that says "rarely indicates cancer but can be a sign of..." and your nervous system stops at the second half. Now you've got a new worst-case scenario. Now you have to Google that. Now you have to look up survival rates. Now you can't stop.

The problem with Googling isn't that the information is wrong. The problem is that your threat-detection system uses scary information as fuel. Googling doesn't reduce uncertainty — it increases it. Every new page is a new rabbit hole. You end up more scared and less informed than when you started.

**Repeating the symptoms to yourself.** You check your body. You feel your lymph nodes. You notice the twinge and you wonder if it's still there. You turn it over in your mind. You check again. The checking behavior tells your nervous system: "Something is wrong enough to check." So your nervous system makes the alarm louder. More checking. More alarm. More checking.

**Seeking reassurance from others.** You ask your partner: "What if it's bad?" They say, "I'm sure it's fine." You feel better for about 45 seconds. Then you ask again, slightly differently. Or you ask someone else. Or you look at their face to see if they're just saying it's fine but they secretly think it's bad.

Reassurance doesn't work because the threat is internal. The uncertainty isn't in the world — it's in your nervous system. Other people can say "you're fine" a hundred times, but your nervous system will just keep generating new reasons to worry. Reassurance is a temporary sugar high, not a solution.

**Mental rehearsal.** We covered this in the last chapter — running through the worst-case scenario doesn't prepare you. It exhausts you.

### What to do instead:

The harder answer — the one nobody wants to hear — is that safety behaviors need to stop. Not because they're bad. But because they feed the loop.

This doesn't mean you ignore symptoms or avoid doctors. It means you stop doing the compulsive behaviors that tell your nervous system there's a crisis to manage.

**The experiment:** For one day, try not Googling. Notice what happens. Notice the urge. Notice what your brain says when you don't Google. See if the world actually ends. It won't. And the absence of new fuel might quiet the fire by a few degrees.

This is not about being tough. It's about recognizing that some of your coping strategies are making it worse.

---

**Key Takeaway:** You are not crazy. Your brain is running predictable threat-assessment programs — pre-grieving, probability distortion, safety behaviors — and they're all working exactly as designed. The problem isn't you. The problem is the mismatch between your ancient alarm system and a world where most scary thoughts don't come true. Knowing the mechanism doesn't make the fear go away. But it gives you something to grab onto when the spiral hits.

You have a life happening right now, not just a hypothetical life that might happen later. And the strategies in this section are about protecting that life — your actual, present, real life — while the uncertainty is still unresolved.

## Chapter 7: Scheduling Your Worry Time

This sounds counterintuitive, but it's one of the most effective tools for managing health anxiety during a waiting period: schedule a time to worry.

Hear us out.

When you're in free-floating anxiety, the worry has no boundaries. It leaks into everything — your commute, your shower, your dinner table, your 2am wake-up. It colonizes your whole life. But when you give worry a scheduled slot, you give it a container. You're telling your nervous system: "I hear you. I'm not ignoring you. But I will address you at this specific time, and until then, I have other things to do."

This works because it validates the worry without letting it run the show.

### How to do it:

**Step 1: Pick a time.** Choose a 20-minute window in your day — usually in the evening works best, before dinner or after. This is your worry time.

**Step 2: Set a reminder.** Put it in your phone. Treat it like an appointment. "Worry Time — 7:30pm."

**Step 3: During your worry time:** Set a timer. Open a notebook or a document. Let yourself worry. Write down what you're afraid of. What you're imagining. What you think is happening. Don't filter it. Don't judge it. Just let it out on paper.

**Step 4: When the timer goes off:** Close the notebook. Walk away. If the worry tries to keep going after the session — and it will — just say, "Not now. I have worry time at 7:30." That's it. That's the boundary.

### Why this works:

Your nervous system needs to feel heard. When you suppress worry entirely, it gets louder — it's like telling a child to be quiet when they're trying to tell you something important. They'll just shout louder. But when you give it a scheduled slot, you say: "Your concern is valid. I will listen to it. But not right now, because right now I have other things that need my attention."

The worry loses its urgency when it knows it has a place to live.

### A practical note:

If you wake up at 2am panicking, you don't have to wait until your scheduled worry time. But you can still use the structure. Say to yourself: "I'm too scared to sleep, and that's real. I'm going to get up. I'm going to ground myself. I'm going to write some of this down. And then I'm going back to bed." You're not suppressing the worry — you're giving it a way out that doesn't involve spiraling for four hours.

## Chapter 8: Protecting Your Sleep (Even When Your Brain Won't Shut Up)

Sleep is the thing that falls apart first during medical wait periods, and it's also the thing your body needs most. When you're exhausted, your emotional regulation capacity drops. Your nervous system runs hotter. The spiral gets faster. So protecting sleep is not self-care woo — it's a functional strategy for staying regulated enough to get through this.

But here's the problem: you can't just decide to sleep. Your brain doesn't take orders from your intentions. So you need to set conditions.

### Practical steps for better sleep during the wait:

**Create a wind-down routine that takes 30 minutes.** Not five minutes — 30. Your brain needs a signal that the day is over. Watch one episode of a show. Read a book. Do something that's not medical-related, not news-related, not outcome-related. The goal is to give your brain a buffer between "day" and "sleep."

**Keep your phone out of the bedroom.** This is non-negotiable. Your phone is where the portal is. It's where you check for results. It's where you Google. If your phone is in the bedroom, every time you can't sleep, you'll reach for it. And the light from the screen tells your brain it's daytime. And the portal is right there. Put the phone in another room. Get a regular alarm clock if you need one.

**If you wake up at 2am and can't get back to sleep within 20 minutes, get up.** Lie there and spiral for two hours is worse than getting up for 30 minutes and going back. Go somewhere uncomfortable — the couch, not the bed. Do something boring. Read something dry. When you feel drowsy, go back to bed. Your brain needs to associate the bed with sleep, not with wakeful anxious rumination.

**Use the physiological sigh (from Part 1) in bed.** If your chest is tight and your mind is racing, two physiological sighs can drop the temperature on the alarm by a few degrees. You're not trying to fall asleep instantly. You're just trying to get the panic signal out of the way so sleep can show up.

## Chapter 9: Knowing What to Tell People (And What to Keep Private)

During medical wait periods, there's a social challenge that nobody talks about: how much do you tell people?

Some people want to tell everyone. They need the support, the distraction, the feeling that people know what they're going through. Other people want to tell no one. They don't want the pity, the questions, the awkward conversations, the well-meaning but exhausting check-ins.

Neither approach is wrong. But there are some things to consider.

### For telling people:

People who love you will want to support you. But they can't support you if they don't know what's happening. If you choose to tell people, try to be specific about what you need: "I don't want advice or opinions. I just want to know you're there. Can we talk about something else?" People can usually respect boundaries when you give them to them.

### For keeping it private:

If you don't want to tell people, that's okay. You don't owe anyone your medical anxiety. But do recognize that carrying it alone has a cost. Find one person — a partner, a friend, a therapist — who you can check in with. You don't have to tell everyone. But you have to tell someone.

### The middle path:

Pick a few people who are good at boundaries. Tell them once. Ask them not to ask you for updates unless you bring it up. Let them know what kind of support you need — distraction, company, silence, someone to vent to. People want to help; they just need to know how.

## Chapter 10: When to Call Your Doctor (And When to Wait)

One of the most anxiety-producing things during a waiting period is not knowing whether to call the office. Did you miss a call? Should you check the portal again? Is the silence good news or bad news? Did something get lost?

Here's a practical framework:

### Call your doctor if:

- You have new or worsening symptoms
- You were told a result would be ready in a specific timeframe and that timeframe has passed by more than a day or two
- You have a question about what the wait means clinically
- You're spiraling and a conversation with a nurse might quiet you down

### Do not call your doctor if:

- You're checking for the third time today
- You want reassurance that everything is okay
- You're hoping they'll "find" your results faster if you call

Here's the thing: results don't come faster because you call. Calling the office doesn't accelerate the lab. And if you call every day, you're feeding your nervous system the message that this is urgent and dangerous. You're practicing the alarm.

If you were told to expect results in 5-7 days, wait 5-7 days. If the timeframe passes and you hear nothing, call once. One call. That's it.

They hold you up while you wait for something real to hold onto.

# Part 4: Living Through It — Maintenance and What Comes Next

## The Long Game

You've read the guide. You know the strategies. But here's what nobody tells you about medical wait periods: the hardest part isn't the first few days. It's the stretch. The days when the initial shock has worn off but there's still no news. The nights when the fear has become background noise. The moments when you think you're doing better and then something triggers you and you're back in the spiral.

This part is about the long game. About how to maintain your regulation when the acute phase is over but the uncertainty is still present. And about what to do when the news comes — whatever the news is.

---

## Chapter 11: Managing the Back-and-Forth

Health anxiety during a waiting period is not linear. You don't just get through the first day and then coast. You oscillate. Some hours you feel almost normal. Some hours you can't breathe. Some days you're productive and engaged and some days you stare at your screen and accomplish nothing.

This is not failure. This is the normal rhythm of uncertainty.

### How to work with the wave:

**Stop trying to maintain a baseline.** Your emotional state will not be stable. That's not a problem to solve — it's a condition to accept. Some days will be worse. Some days will be better. Your job is not to feel good every day. Your job is to not lose yourself in the bad days.

**Track your triggers.** Pay attention to the moments when the spiral gets worse. Is it the morning? When you see a certain person? When you check the portal? When you get bad news about something unrelated? Knowing your triggers doesn't eliminate them — but it gives you a head start. You can prepare. You can do extra grounding before a trigger hits.

**Give yourself grace on bad days.** If you had a day where you couldn't focus, where you scrolled for three hours, where you cried, where you did none of the things you planned — that's okay. One bad day is not a collapse. It's a rough day. The difference matters. A rough day is something that happens. A collapse is something you make mean something about yourself.

## Chapter 12: When the News Arrives

Eventually, the call comes. Or the results show up in the portal. Or the appointment arrives. The waiting is over.

What happens next depends on what the news is. But there are a few things worth saying regardless.

### If the news is good:

Celebrate. Really. This is harder than it sounds because your nervous system has been running threat-detection for days or weeks, and it doesn't just flip off like a switch. You might feel relief and also residual fear. You might keep checking. You might not trust it.

This is normal. The anxiety won't disappear the moment you get good news. It will fade — over days, sometimes over weeks. Be patient with yourself. And if the anxiety persists for more than a few weeks after you've received clear results, consider talking to someone. A therapist, specifically someone who works with health anxiety, can help you re-regulate a nervous system that's been stuck in threat mode.

### If the news is not what you hoped for:

This is not the part of the guide where we tell you it will be okay. It might not be. What we can tell you is this: you got through the waiting. You built a toolkit. You know how to regulate your nervous system under pressure. Those skills don't go away when the stakes get higher — they become more necessary.

And you will find out what you need to find out. You will learn the plan. You will take the next step. It will be hard and it will be scary and it will be nothing like what you imagined when you were pre-grieving in the middle of the night. Because the actual thing is never the same as the catastrophe.

One step at a time. That's all anyone's ever done.
