## Chapter 1: Draft a Payment Hierarchy Agreement

Here's something most people never learn until it's too late: verbal agreements about money aren't worth the breath it takes to speak them. Not because your roommate is a liar—maybe they are, maybe they aren't—but because memory is malleable and human beings are exceptional at reconstructing the past to suit the present.

A Payment Hierarchy Agreement is your first line of defense. It's not a lease addendum (you'll get to that in Part 2). It's a one-page handshake-to-paper upgrade that establishes clear financial expectations before money becomes an issue.

### What This Document Does

Think of it as a financial conversation that happened once and got recorded. It specifies:

- **Payment order**: Which bills get paid first when money is tight. Rent always comes first. Then utilities. Then shared household items. This matters because "we're both broke" feels equal until you realize their "broke" includes a streaming subscription while your "broke" means potential eviction.

- **Due dates with grace period definitions**: "First of the month" isn't specific enough. Specify the exact date, the exact time (end of business day? midnight?), and what the grace period is—48 hours? Five business days? Name it explicitly. The grace period isn't generosity; it's accounting for the reality that banks process transfers on their schedule, not yours.

- **Late payment consequences**: Not moral consequences ("you should feel bad")—financial ones. A flat $25 late fee. Interest after 15 days. Something concrete that makes the cost of lateness visible. The moment you quantify the consequence, you remove the awkwardness of having to ask for it.

### The Format

Keep it simple. One page. Here's the skeleton:

**[Your Name] and [Roommate Name] Payment Agreement**

Date: _______________

Rent Due: _________ (date) by _________ (time)
Grace Period: _________ days

Late Fee: $_________ per _________

Payment Order (if funds are limited):
1. Rent
2. Electric/Gas
3. Internet
4. [Other shared bills]

We agree to these terms and understand that late payment will result in the specified consequences.

Signature: _____________________ Date: ___________
Signature: _____________________ Date: ___________

### Your Action Step for Today

Download or copy this template. Fill it in with your actual numbers. Print two copies. Sign both. Give one to your roommate. Keep one somewhere safe. This takes twenty minutes and creates a documented agreement that didn't exist before.

**Key Takeaway:** A Payment Hierarchy Agreement transforms vague money conversations into documented commitments. The act of writing it down—of making both parties sign—does more work than the language itself. It forces a real acknowledgment of what's expected, and that acknowledgment changes behavior.

## Chapter 2: Open a Joint Rent Account with Auto-Split

The Venmo problem is real. "I'll Venmo you" sounds casual and friendly until you're checking your phone at 11 PM wondering if that $400 transfer actually happened or if you're about to be short for rent. Venmo is great for splitting dinner. It's a disaster for shared rent.

Here's the system that eliminates the chase entirely: a joint rent account with auto-split contributions.

### How It Works

1. **Open a separate checking account** at a bank you already use (or a credit union, which often has lower fees). Name it something obvious like "Apartment Rent Account." This isn't your personal money—it's a neutral vessel.

2. **Set up direct deposit or automatic transfers** from each roommate's personal accounts to this rent account on a specific date—ideally 5-7 days before rent is actually due. This gives you a buffer for processing.

3. **Each person's contribution goes in separately**, clearly labeled with their name. When rent is due, the account pays the landlord directly via the payment method they accept (check, ACH, online portal—whatever it is).

4. **The moment rent clears**, you screenshot the confirmation. Both roommates get a copy. Done.

### Why This Eliminates the Problem

No hand-offs. No "I'll get it to you." No chasing. If your roommate hasn't deposited their share by the deadline, the account shows it immediately. There's no gray area about whether they paid—they either did or they didn't, and it's documented.

This also protects your roommate, by the way. If you pay them cash and you forget to apply it to rent, they have no proof. This system creates a clean record for everyone.

### The Auto-Split Option

Some banks and apps (Chime, Ally, even some credit unions) let you set up automatic splits where each person's direct deposit can be automatically allocated—say, 60% to one account and 40% to another. This is even better because it removes the human element entirely. Money arrives, gets sorted, rent gets paid. No one has to remember anything.

### Your Action Step for Today

Check your bank's website or app for joint account options. Most checking accounts don't have monthly fees if you meet minimum balance requirements, and some credit unions offer free shared accounts. Figure out what it would cost you per month (probably $5-15), compare that to the $2,500 you're trying to protect, and open the account this week.

**Key Takeaway:** Joint rent accounts eliminate the "I'll pay you back" gray area. Money moves automatically, rent gets paid directly, and both roommates have verifiable records. The cost is minimal; the protection is substantial.

## Chapter 3: Create a Payment Confirmation Protocol

Every financial transaction between roommates needs to be documented, dated, and retrievable. Not because you're expecting fraud—but because memory is unreliable and documentation is objective.

A Payment Confirmation Protocol is simply an agreement about how you'll record and store proof of every payment. It takes the uncertainty out of "did we handle that?" and replaces it with "here's the record."

### The System

**Step 1: Screenshots for Every Transfer**

Every time your roommate pays you—or you pay rent from the joint account—you take a screenshot. Not just of the transaction confirmation, but of any relevant context: the amount, the date, the notes field. Save these in a dedicated folder on your phone called "Rent [Month Year]" or use Google Photos with a consistent tag.

**Step 2: A Shared Spreadsheet**

Create a shared Google Sheet (or Excel, if you prefer) with columns for:

- Date
- Amount Due
- Amount Paid
- Payment Method
- Confirmation Number/Proof
- Status (Paid in Full / Partial / Owed)

Both roommates get edit access. Both can add rows. This creates a living document that shows the complete financial history at a glance.

**Step 3: A Communication Log**

For any conversation about money—reminders, disputes, agreements—keep a record. Text messages count. Emails count. If you have an in-person conversation about payment, follow it up with a text summary: "Hey, just confirming we agreed that you'll have the $400 to me by Friday the 15th." Their response becomes documentation.

### Why This Matters More Than You Think

Here's the uncomfortable truth: people genuinely misremember financial details, especially under stress. Your roommate might honestly believe they paid more than they did—or might reconstruct the past in ways that feel true to them. Documentation prevents these "he said/she said" moments because the record exists independently of anyone's memory or intentions.

This also makes it much easier to have the conversation. Instead of "I feel like you're always late," you can say "According to the spreadsheet, March and April payments came in 8 and 11 days late, which triggered the late fees we agreed on." Facts defuse arguments.

### Your Action Step for Today

Open a new Google Sheet. Label it "Roommate Payment Log." Add the columns listed above. Share it with your roommate. Send them a quick text: "Made a shared spreadsheet to track all payments—figure it'll make everything clearer for both of us. Can you check your email to accept the invite?" That's it. You've started the system.

**Key Takeaway:** A Payment Confirmation Protocol creates verifiable evidence for every transaction. When everything is documented, disputes become impossible—or at least very short. The spreadsheet isn't surveillance; it's clarity that protects both of you.

---

**Part 1 Key Takeaway:**

Documenting financial agreements before problems escalate is the single highest-leverage thing you can do as a renter with roommates. A Payment Hierarchy Agreement establishes clear expectations. A joint rent account eliminates hand-off risk. A Payment Confirmation Protocol creates verifiable records. Together, these systems make late payments immediately visible, consequences automatic, and disputes preventable. Start all three today—even if you only have twenty minutes.

## Chapter 4: Draft a Roommate Agreement with Financial Clauses

Your lease protects your landlord. Your verbal roommate understanding protects no one. What you need is a standalone document—separate from the lease—that addresses the financial relationship between you and your roommate with the seriousness it deserves.

A Roommate Agreement with Financial Clauses is a contract. It's not a lease addendum (you'll work on that with your landlord in the next chapter), and it's not legally binding in the way a signed commercial contract is. But it's strong enough to support a case if you ever need to pursue legal action—and the act of creating it often prevents the need for legal action entirely.

### What Goes in This Agreement

**1. Financial Responsibilities (By Line Item)**

Don't say "utilities." Say "electricity, gas, internet, and water, split [50/50 / proportional to square footage / some other basis]." Be specific about which streaming services, cleaning supplies, or other shared costs are included. Ambiguity here creates conflicts later.

**2. Income Loss Provisions**

What happens if your roommate loses their job? This is the clause most people skip—and regret later. Include language like:

*"If a roommate experiences involuntary job loss, they must notify the other roommate(s) within 72 hours. A payment plan may be negotiated, but the non-paying party remains legally responsible for their share. Failure to communicate within 14 days constitutes breach of this agreement."*

This isn't cruel. It's honest. It gives your roommate an incentive to talk to you early, when something can be worked out, rather than waiting until the damage is done.

**3. Breach Definitions**

Define what "breach" means in concrete terms. Is it one missed payment? Two consecutive? A payment more than 30 days late? Write it down. Vague breach definitions don't hold up to scrutiny.

**4. Exit Clauses (30/60/90)**

Both parties need a path out that doesn't leave anyone blindsided. Standard language:

- *30-day notice*: Either party may terminate this agreement with 30 days written notice. The departing party remains responsible for rent through the end of the notice period.
- *60-day transition*: If a roommate plans to leave, they agree to cooperate in finding a replacement and will not unreasonably withhold consent for a qualified applicant.
- *90-day vacancy responsibility*: If no replacement is found, the departing roommate remains financially responsible for their share for up to 90 days, or until a replacement is found, whichever comes first.

These timelines give everyone breathing room while ensuring the departing party can't simply walk away mid-lease.

### Your Action Step for Today

Find a free roommate agreement template online (Nolo and Rocket Lawyer both offer basic versions). Customize it with the specific financial details from your Payment Hierarchy Agreement in Part 1. Print two copies. Sign both. Exchange them. Store one somewhere safe.

**Key Takeaway:** A Roommate Agreement transforms informal understandings into documented commitments. The exit clauses alone are worth the effort—they give both parties a clear path out and prevent the "wait, you're leaving?!" conversation that leaves one roommate holding the bag.

## Chapter 5: Add a Formal Amendment to Your Lease

Here's something most renters don't realize: your lease probably has a clause about roommate arrangements, and you might be able to modify it to add financial protection. This requires a conversation with your property manager or landlord, but the conversation is often easier than people expect—if you know what to ask for.

### Why Amend the Lease?

A lease amendment creates legally binding obligations that survive your informal roommate agreement. If your roommate defaults and you've documented everything, the landlord may be able to help—or at least won't be able to hold you solely responsible if they've been formally notified.

### What to Ask For

**Option 1: Financial Responsibility Clause**

Request language that clarifies your liability if your roommate fails to pay. Something like: "Tenant [Your Name] is responsible for [50%/their portion] of monthly rent. The remaining [50%/their portion] is the responsibility of [Roommate Name], and [Your Name] will not be held liable for their portion in the event of default."

This isn't always granted, but many landlords will add it—especially if you have good payment history.

**Option 2: Subletting/Replacement Tenant Clause**

If your roommate leaves or defaults, this clause gives you the right to find a replacement without going through the full application process from scratch. It speeds up the transition significantly.

**Option 3: Formal Acknowledgment of Roommate**

Some leases require all occupants to be on the lease. If your roommate isn't on the lease, they're technically an unauthorized occupant. Getting them added—or at least formally acknowledged—creates accountability.

### How to Ask

Here's a template email to your property manager:

---

**Subject: Request to Add Financial Clause to Lease**

Hi [Property Manager Name],

I'm reaching out because I'd like to add a financial responsibility clause to my lease to clarify the payment arrangement between myself and my roommate, [Roommate Name].

Currently, we split rent [50/50 / X% and Y%], with each of us responsible for our portion. I'd like the lease to reflect that I'm only liable for my share, and that [Roommate Name]'s share is their separate obligation.

I'm a [X-year] tenant with [history, e.g., "no late payments and consistent rent paid on time"]. I'd appreciate the opportunity to discuss whether this amendment is possible.

Would you have time to chat this week? Happy to come by your office or schedule a call.

Thanks,
[Your Name]
[Unit Number]

---

### Your Action Step for Today

Send this email today. The worst they can say is no, and "no" is information. If they say yes, you'll have formal legal protection. If they say no, you'll know where you stand and can plan accordingly. Either way, you'll have started the conversation.

**Key Takeaway:** A lease amendment creates legally binding financial distinctions between roommates. Even if your landlord declines to add the language, the conversation opens doors you didn't know existed—including the possibility of formalizing your roommate arrangement in other ways.

## Chapter 6: Document Everything for Eviction Prep (Without Starting One)

Here's a phrase that will keep you up at night: "We were roommates. I wasn't on the lease. And now I might get evicted because of someone else's debt."

This chapter is about preparation, not action. You hope you'll never need this. But knowing how to document for a potential eviction proceeding gives you power—and often prevents the need to use it.

### What "Eviction Prep" Actually Means

When a tenant doesn't pay rent, landlords can begin eviction proceedings. If you're both on the lease, you're both potentially liable—landlords often pursue the tenant who's easiest to reach, which is usually whoever answers the phone. Even if the problem is your roommate's unpaid half, you could face eviction if you can't make up the difference.

Eviction prep documentation protects you by proving that you paid your share, communicated the issue, and took reasonable steps to resolve it. It shows the landlord (and potentially a court) that you're not the problem.

### The Documentation Package

**1. Payment Records**

Every payment you've made, with proof. Bank statements, screenshots, canceled checks. Ideally organized by month, with your portion clearly identified.

**2. Communication Logs**

Every conversation about rent, organized chronologically. Texts, emails, notes from in-person meetings. Include dates, summaries of what was discussed, and any follow-up messages confirming agreements.

**3. Written Notices**

If your roommate is late or short, document it with a written notice—even if you also text them. The notice should include:

- The date
- The amount owed
- The deadline for payment
- Consequences if unpaid

Keep a copy. Send one via email (which creates a timestamp) and one via text (which is faster).

**4. Your Attempts to Resolve**

If you've tried to work out a payment plan, negotiate, or help your roommate find resources, document those attempts. Screenshots of job listings you sent them. Notes from conversations about local assistance programs. This shows good faith.

### The Difference Between Giving Notice and Starting Proceedings

This is critical: giving your roommate written notice that they're in breach is not the same as starting eviction proceedings. Notice is a step toward resolution. Eviction is a legal action. The line between them varies by state, but generally:

- **Notice**: "You're late on rent. You have X days to pay or we need to discuss next steps." This is standard roommate communication.
- **Demand for Compliance**: "Per our agreement, this constitutes breach. If not resolved by [date], I will pursue available remedies." This is escalation but still pre-legal.
- **Eviction Filing**: This is court. This is formal. This has legal consequences for your roommate and is not easily undone.

Most situations resolve at the notice or demand stage. But having the documentation ready means you're prepared for whatever comes.

### Your Action Step for Today

Create a folder—physical or digital—labeled "Eviction Prep Documentation." Start adding your payment records. Add your communication log. If you're already behind on documentation, start today. It takes thirty minutes to begin and creates a record that could save your housing.

**Key Takeaway:** Eviction prep documentation protects you by proving you did your part. You don't need to start anything—you just need to be ready. Payment records, communication logs, written notices, and evidence of good-faith resolution attempts create a package that supports your position if things go legal.

## Chapter 7: Send a Formal Payment Demand Letter

You have the documentation. You have the conversations. You've texted reminders, maybe even had the awkward in-person talk. And still, that $2,500 sits there like a silent accusation.

It's time to move from informal requests to formal demand. A Payment Demand Letter is the bridge between "please pay me" and "I'm taking you to court." It sounds aggressive. It isn't—not really. It's just clear.

### Why a Letter Works When Texts Don't

Texts are informal. They're easy to ignore. They can be read in a dozen different ways depending on the reader's mood. A formal letter—physical or electronic—has weight. It signals that you're serious. It creates a documented timeline. And if it ends up in a legal proceeding, it's evidence that you made a clear, specific demand before escalating.

### How to Write It

**The Header**

Include your name, your roommate's name, the date, and a clear subject line: "FORMAL PAYMENT DEMAND – RENT OWED."

**The Opening Statement**

Get to the point immediately:

*"This letter constitutes a formal demand for payment of $2,500 in owed rent and shared expenses, which you have failed to pay despite multiple verbal and written requests."*

**The Breakdown**

List what they're owed, with specifics:

- March rent shortfall: $287.50 (paid March 8, 12 days late)
- April rent shortfall: $287.50 (partial payment of $200, balance unpaid)
- May rent shortfall: $287.50 (not paid)
- Utility shortfalls: [list amounts]
- Late fees (per our agreement): [calculated amount]
- **Total Owed: $2,500**

**The Deadline**

Give them a specific date—typically 10 to 14 days from the letter's date. Make it firm.

*"Payment must be received by [DATE] at [TIME]. Payment should be made via [your preferred method—bank transfer, check, etc.] with confirmation sent to [your email/address]."*

**The Consequences**

State clearly what happens if they don't pay:

*"If payment is not received by [DATE], I will pursue all available legal remedies, including filing in small claims court and providing this documentation to the landlord as evidence of breach of our roommate agreement. You will also be responsible for court costs and any additional collection costs."*

**The Signature**

Sign it. Print it. Send it via two methods:

1. **Email** (for speed and timestamp)
2. **Certified mail with return receipt** (for legal weight)

Keep the certified mail receipt. Keep a copy of everything.

### Your Action Step for Today

Draft this letter using your actual numbers. Don't soften it—this is not the moment for diplomatic phrasing. Send it today via email. Mail the certified copy tomorrow. Set a calendar reminder for the deadline date.

**Key Takeaway:** A Payment Demand Letter escalates the conversation from informal request to documented ultimatum. It's not hostile—it's clear. It gives your roommate one last chance to make it right, and it creates the legal foundation for the next step if they don't.

## Chapter 8: Negotiate a Payment Plan Without Feeling Like a Jerk

Here's something most people get wrong: demanding payment and being willing to negotiate aren't opposites. They're sequential. You send the demand letter, and if your roommate responds with genuine effort—explanation, apology, proposal—you have the option to work with them.

The key is knowing the difference between someone who's struggling and someone who's stalling.

### The Legitimate Struggle vs. the Pattern of Excuse

A legitimate financial crisis has texture:

- They lost a job, faced a medical emergency, had a family death
- They've communicated early and honestly
- They're proposing concrete solutions—payment dates, amounts, a specific timeline
- They're willing to put the agreement in writing

A pattern of excuse has a different texture:

- The explanations keep changing ("I lost my job" becomes "I had unexpected expenses" becomes "I thought we worked something out")
- The apologies are profuse but the money never arrives
- The proposals are vague or keep getting revised
- They avoid committing to writing

If you're dealing with the first pattern, a payment plan might serve everyone. If you're dealing with the second, the payment plan is just another thing they'll break.

### How to Structure a Payment Plan

If you decide to negotiate, here's the framework:

**1. Acknowledge the Debt in Writing**

*"We agree that [Roommate Name] owes [Amount] to [Your Name], to be repaid according to the following schedule..."*

Both parties sign. This converts the verbal promise into a documented commitment.

**2. Set Specific Payments**

Don't say "what can you afford?" That's vague and easily renegotiated. Ask for specifics: "What amount can you pay by [date]?" If they can't give you a concrete number, that's your answer.

**3. Include Consequences for Default**

*"If a payment is missed or returned for insufficient funds, the remaining balance becomes immediately due, and [Your Name] reserves the right to pursue legal remedies without further notice."*

This is the clause that makes the plan enforceable.

**4. Keep the Demand Letter in Your Pocket**

Send it, keep the certified copy, but don't file it yet. If they follow the payment plan, you never need to use it. If they miss a payment, you have the demand letter ready to go.

### Your Action Step for Today

If you haven't sent the demand letter yet, send it today. If you've sent it and your roommate has responded with a proposal, evaluate it honestly against the patterns above. If it looks genuine, respond within 24 hours with a counter-proposal in writing. If it looks like more stalling, move to the next chapter.

**Key Takeaway:** A payment plan is an option, not an obligation. If your roommate demonstrates genuine effort—early communication, concrete proposals, willingness to document—you can work with them. But stay alert for the pattern of excuse, and keep your demand letter ready to deploy if the plan falls apart.

## Chapter 9: Take Them to Small Claims Court (And Win)

If you've sent the demand letter, given them a chance to negotiate, and they're still not paying—it's time to consider small claims court.

Here's the good news: small claims court is designed for exactly this situation. It's informal. It's fast. It doesn't require a lawyer. And with good documentation, you can win.

### When to File

File small claims when:

- The amount owed is within your state's limit (typically $3,000 to $15,000 depending on the state—check yours)
- You have documented evidence of the debt (the demand letter, payment records, communication logs, the signed roommate agreement)
- Your roommate hasn't filed for bankruptcy (which would freeze collections)
- You've exhausted informal options

Don't file if you don't have documentation. The judge needs evidence, not your word.

### How to File

**Step 1: Check Your State's Process**

Search "[Your State] small claims court filing" for the specific procedure. Most states have online filing options.

**Step 2: Prepare Your Documentation**

Organize everything chronologically:

- The original Payment Hierarchy Agreement
- The Roommate Agreement with Financial Clauses
- Payment records showing what you paid vs. what they paid
- The demand letter with proof of delivery
- Communication logs showing attempts to resolve
- Any payment plan proposals (theirs and yours)

Make three copies: one for the judge, one for yourself, one for the defendant.

**Step 3: File the Claim**

You'll pay a filing fee—typically $30 to $150 depending on the claim amount. This is recoverable if you win.

**Step 4: Serve the Defendant**

The defendant must be formally notified. Your state will specify acceptable methods (personal service, certified mail, sheriff's service). Follow the rules exactly—defective service can get your case dismissed.

**Step 5: Show Up**

Small claims hearings are usually scheduled within a few weeks of filing. Show up on time, bring your documentation, and present your case clearly:

- "We had an agreement."
- "Here's the documentation."
- "I demanded payment on [date]."
- "They have not paid."

That's it. The judge will ask questions. Answer them directly. Don't ramble. Don't attack. Just present facts.

### What Happens If You Win

A judgment in your favor is a court order requiring payment. If your roommate pays voluntarily, you're done. If they don't, you can pursue collection methods—wage garnishment, bank levy, property liens—though these vary by state and can take additional legal steps.

### What Happens If You Lose

You can appeal in some states, but you need grounds (procedural error, new evidence). Losing is uncommon if you have solid documentation, but it's possible if the judge finds ambiguity in your agreements or your evidence is weak.

### Your Action Step for Today

Check your state's small claims limit and filing process right now. If your $2,500 is within the limit and you've sent the demand letter without response, you're ready to file. The process takes a few hours and costs less than $100. Do it.

**Key Takeaway:** Small claims court is your backstop when everything else fails. It's designed to be accessible without a lawyer, and with good documentation, you have a strong case. Filing isn't burning bridges—it's enforcing an agreement your roommate agreed to but broke. If the money is owed, a judge should agree.

---

**Part 3 Key Takeaway:**

Collecting what's owed is a sequential process: formal demand letter, good-faith negotiation if warranted, small claims court if neither works. Each step builds on the last and creates documentation for the next. You don't have to start with court—you start with clarity. And you move forward with evidence.

## Chapter 10: How to Give 30-Day Notice (Without Apologizing)

There comes a point where the math doesn't work anymore. The relationship is damaged. The money isn't coming back. And staying in this arrangement is costing you more than leaving it.

If you've decided to end the roommate situation—whether that means your roommate leaves, you leave, or you find a replacement—the 30-day notice is your tool. Here's how to do it without guilt, drama, or legal exposure.

### The Notice Itself

A 30-day notice should be written, not verbal. It should include:

- The date you're giving notice
- The date the notice expires (30 days from delivery, or whatever your agreement specifies)
- Your intention regarding the living arrangement
- Your share of any final obligations

Here's a template:

---

**[Your Name] to [Roommate Name]**
**Formal Notice of Intent to Terminate Roommate Arrangement**

Date: _______________

This letter serves as formal notice that I intend to [leave the shared residence / end our roommate arrangement] effective [30 days from date above].

Final financial obligations:
- Rent through [end date]: $[amount]
- Utilities through [end date]: $[amount]
- Other: $[amount]

I will pay my share of these obligations by [date]. I expect the same.

I am willing to cooperate in finding a replacement roommate or coordinating a transition plan. Please contact me at [contact info] to discuss logistics.

[Signature]

---

### Deliver It via Certified Mail

Don't just hand it to them. Send a copy via certified mail with return receipt. This creates proof of delivery with a timestamp. If they claim they never received it, you have evidence.

### The Guilt Trap

You might feel bad. That's okay. But ask yourself: is this feeling based on what you actually owe them, or on an idea that you shouldn't protect yourself?

You don't owe your roommate unlimited patience. You don't owe them the continued use of your money while they figure things out. You don't owe them your housing stability. What you owe them is the notice you're legally and contractually obligated to provide—and you're providing it.

### Your Action Step for Today

Decide what you're going to do: are you leaving, or are they? Once you know, draft the notice. Send it today. Set a calendar reminder for the move-out date and any associated deadlines.

**Key Takeaway:** Giving notice isn't burning someone. It's honoring your commitment by doing it formally and on time. The notice protects you legally and gives your roommate clarity. You can feel compassion for their situation while still protecting your own.

## Chapter 11: Find a Better Roommate Next Time

If you're going to have roommates again, learn from this. The next time, set up systems that make the financial relationship clear from day one—before anyone moves in, before any money changes hands.

### The筛选 Process

Most people pick roommates the way they pick friends: vibes. This is backwards. Screen for financial responsibility:

**1. Income Verification**

Ask for proof of income—pay stubs, bank statements, an offer letter. Not because you're auditing them, but because you need to know their rent-to-income ratio. If rent is more than 40% of their income, that's a red flag.

**2. Credit Check**

You're allowed to run a credit check with their permission. A low credit score doesn't disqualify someone, but it tells you something about their relationship with financial obligations.

**3. Reference Check**

Ask for references—previous landlords, not friends. Call them. Ask: "Did they pay on time? Would you rent to them again?"

**4. The Money Conversation Up Front**

Before they sign anything or give any money, have a direct conversation about financial expectations. Use your Payment Hierarchy Agreement template. Walk through it together. Answer their questions. Watch how they respond to structure—if they resist, that's information.

### The Onboarding System

Once you've selected someone, onboard them like you'd onboard an employee:

- Walk through the Payment Hierarchy Agreement
- Show them the shared spreadsheet
- Explain the joint rent account (or set one up together)
- Review the late fee policy
- Show them the communication log system

This isn't cold. It's professional. It sets expectations. And it tells them you're serious, which attracts serious people.

### Your Action Step for Today

If you're currently between roommates, draft your screening checklist now. Include income verification, credit check authorization, landlord references, and a list of questions about their financial habits. Have it ready for the next conversation.

**Key Takeaway:** Good roommates are found, not hoped for. A screening process with income verification, reference checks, and direct financial conversations filters out problems before they move in. The onboarding process sets expectations that prevent problems after.
